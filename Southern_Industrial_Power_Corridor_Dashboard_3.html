Aluminium: The Anchor Industry for Pakistan's Southern Power Corridor

Aluminium is "solid electricity" — a metal whose cost structure is dominated by the price of power. Pakistan's stranded southern generation and Gadani's scrap base give it a rare, two-track route into the global aluminium trade: a fast, low-capital secondary/recycling anchor now, and a strategic primary-smelting option later.

The case for aluminium

Why aluminium is the right first anchor, not just an eligible one

Aluminium is unusual among traded commodities: its production cost is a direct, calculable function of the electricity price. That makes it the cleanest possible test of whether cheap southern power can be converted into a globally competitive export industry — and it gives Pakistan two separate entry points, not one.

Route 1 — Secondary aluminium (recycling)

Uses 400–800 kWh/tonne — about 95% less electricity than primary smelting. Electricity is only 8–15% of production cost; scrap is the dominant input. This is already viable at Pakistan's current energy charge (before GST and surcharges) when fed with domestic and Gadani-sourced scrap. Low capital intensity, fast to bankability, and the recommended first-wave anchor for the corridor.

Route 2 — Primary aluminium (smelting)

Uses 13,000–15,000 kWh/tonne via Hall-Héroult electrolysis — the most electricity-intensive process in commercial manufacturing, at 40–45% of total production cost. Genuinely "solid electricity." Requires a delivered tariff at or below USD 0.040/kWh, a 15–25 year supply contract, and a credible global smelter operator. A strategic, second-wave anchor — not a near-term mass-market target.

The strategic logic
Because electricity is such a large share of aluminium's cost, no other industry converts a cheap southern tariff into competitiveness as directly or as visibly. Getting the aluminium tariff right is therefore also a proof point for every other energy-intensive sector the corridor is meant to serve.
The enabling resource

The power platform aluminium would draw on

Aluminium's tariff case only works because there is real, measured, underutilized generation to sell it from. Three southern plants — CPHGCL, LEPCL, PQEPCL — are paid for whether they run or not, and are running at a fraction of their design capacity.

Actual generation vs. 85% utilization benchmark (GWh/year)

18.73 TWh/year of measured dispatch headroom across the three plants — enough, at 85% load factor, to supply the full corridor including its aluminium allocation many times over.

Capacity cost per kWh: current vs. 85% utilization

Higher utilization compresses the plants' capacity cost from PKR 68.75/kWh to PKR 13.08/kWh (weighted average) — the mechanism that makes a low delivered tariff to aluminium consistent with, rather than a subsidy against, the rest of the power system.

This is not a free-power proposal. Aluminium offtakers would pay a tariff above the plants' marginal generation cost (roughly PKR 8–14/kWh depending on fuel case), with the margin credited back to the national capacity-payment pool through a settlement mechanism — see Allocation & Roadmap.

At 600 kWh/t, this implies viability up to ~USD 0.167/kWh with a domestic scrap blend — meaning secondary aluminium is already viable at Pakistan's current energy charge; GST and surcharges, not the tariff itself, are the obstacle.

Primary aluminium: optimal tariff rises with the LME cycle

A smelter sized to the mid-cycle price (USD 2,600/t) looks viable near USD 0.099/kWh — but fails at trough. The through-cycle threshold the Aluminum Association cites, and the one Saudi Arabia's Ma'aden operates against, is USD 0.040/kWh.

EBITDA per tonne: Saudi tariff vs. Pakistan's current tariff

At Pakistan's current all-in rate, a primary smelter is at breakeven even at mid-cycle LME prices, and loses roughly USD 585/tonne at trough. At Saudi's USD 0.040/kWh, the same smelter remains profitable through the full cycle.

Secondary aluminium is Pakistan's lowest-hanging fruit in the aluminium value chain. The tariff requirement is achievable now, the domestic and Gadani scrap strategy is viable, and the regional market for alloy ingots and extrusion billets is proven. This is why it is recommended as the first aluminium anchor investment in the corridor, with primary aluminium held as a strategic, conditional second stage.

Beyond the smelter gate

The economic multiplier: value added, jobs, exports

Aluminium's GDP contribution does not come from selling cheap power. It comes from converting that power into value added, and the multiplier is highest downstream — in extrusion, fabrication, and finished products — not at the primary smelting stage itself.

Value-added multiplier by stage

Every USD 1 of primary/secondary smelting output supports roughly USD 1.5–2.0 of total linked economic activity; every USD 1 of downstream fabrication (extrusion, profiles, cable) supports roughly USD 2.5–3.5. This is the case for pairing primary/secondary capacity with a downstream park from day one, not building smelting alone.

Employment: first-wave secondary aluminium anchor (100,000 t/yr)

Direct jobs at the plant are a small share of total employment. The downstream conversion layer — extrusion, packaging, wire, appliance and auto components — generates 5–8 times more jobs than the primary process alone.

Export revenue

USD 180–220 million/year from a first-wave 100,000 t/year secondary aluminium anchor (alloy ingots, profiles, extrusions) into GCC and regional markets.

Indicative FDI

USD 80–120 million for a first anchor secondary-aluminium investment — part of a broader 3–4 anchor-project pipeline the corridor is designed to attract across sectors.

Import substitution

Aluminium profiles, extrusions, and packaging are already imported at scale. Domestic production at a competitive tariff displaces that import bill directly — demand for the product already exists.

What must not happen

Pricing primary aluminium off a mid-cycle LME assumption. Granting the tariff without a locked incremental-load baseline. Allowing the fixed-cost contribution to bypass the national capacity-payment pool. Committing to a world-scale primary smelter before a global operator and 15–25 year supply contract are in place.

The sequencing exists precisely to avoid these failure modes: prove the mechanism on secondary aluminium, where capital and technical risk are low, before committing to the primary module.

Bottom line
Aluminium is the sharpest test of the corridor's central claim: that already-paid-for southern power, priced above marginal cost and below today's punitive tariff, can make a genuinely electricity-intensive industry competitive. Secondary aluminium proves the mechanism within 12–24 months at low capital risk. Primary aluminium, done right, turns that proof into a strategic industrial anchor.
