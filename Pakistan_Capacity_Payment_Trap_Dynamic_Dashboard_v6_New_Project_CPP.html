Executive Summary
The denominator crisis: fixed costs rise while recoverable units become fragile
Pakistan’s power-sector crisis is no longer only a supply-addition problem. It is a fixed-cost recovery problem. The system can satisfy energy adequacy and peak adequacy on paper, yet fail tariff adequacy if behind-the-meter solar, household batteries, weak industrial demand, losses, and collections reduce recoverable billed units. This dashboard therefore tests four dimensions together: distributed energy, cooling peaks, IGCEP/TSEP capital cost, and the tariff denominator.

Policy reading of the model
Base risk
If demand grows according to IGCEP but recoverable units are eroded by DER adoption and losses, tariffs rise even if the generation plan is least-cost in resource-planning terms.
Critical denominator lever
High-load-factor industrial demand is a tariff-reduction instrument because it adds kWh without proportionately adding capacity obligations.
Architecture fix
Strategic public-good infrastructure can be partly shifted to PSDP, concessional finance, climate finance, or a sovereign infrastructure vehicle rather than fully loaded into consumer tariffs.
Solar Projections
Behind-the-meter solar continues to grow, reducing daytime grid demand and recoverable sales
The solar tab links import data to cumulative BTM capacity and then to grid impact. The projection file shows cumulative raw solar capacity rising from 45 GW in 2024 to 174.6 GW by 2035, with effective degraded capacity of about 167.9 GW. The system implication is not simply more cheap energy: it is lower daytime grid offtake, net-metering settlements, and a more severe ramp between solar hours and evening/midnight cooling demand.
Methodology step 1
Imports → annual MW
Annual imported kW is converted to MW and import value using observed/imported module prices. Actual 2024 is treated as the stock jump year; later years use projection growth.
Methodology step 2
Cumulative capacity → effective capacity
Cumulative raw MW is adjusted for degradation, producing an effective capacity series that is more relevant for generation and grid impact.
Methodology step 3
Solar energy → denominator erosion
BTM self-consumption and net-metering export adjustments are deducted from gross underlying demand to estimate grid-visible and recoverable units.

Battery Projections
Distributed batteries can shave peak, but also reduce billed purchases
The battery model is value-based because customs data reports import value more reliably than usable kWh. Imports are converted to GWh using an assumed US$/kWh path. The projection uses FY2025-26 as the base year, a central battery price of US$84/kWh, and price elasticity of demand of 1.5. Prices fall toward US$51/kWh by FY2034-35, causing annual GWh imported to rise faster than the import bill.

Cooling Demand
Cooling demand is structural, income-elastic, and peaks after sunset
The cooling model uses the power system as the control total. FY2024-25 ISMO hourly demand shows a February non-cooling baseline around 9.95 GW, a June peak of 23.82 GW ex-KE, and a system cooling peak around 16 GW including KE. The model then explains this control total through fan stock, AC effective stock, residual commercial/institutional cooling, losses, and BTM-hidden daytime cooling.

Planning implication: Solar helps daytime energy, but Pakistan’s observed cooling peak occurs around midnight. Without inverter AC standards, building efficiency, thermal storage, ToU demand response and appliance financing, cooling will continue to drive capacity need even as solar erodes daytime billed sales.
IGCEP
Generation expansion is least-cost only if demand and load factor materialize
IGCEP 2025–35 is not only a generation-addition plan; it is a signal that Pakistan’s planning problem has shifted toward load shape, net metering, DSM and industrial base-load restoration. Low BAU gross energy reaches 180.6 TWh by 2035, but grid-supplied net energy after net metering is only 167.3 TWh. Peak remains 35.5 GW in Low BAU, while Low DSM cuts 2035 peak to 28.6 GW without reducing annual energy.

TSEP
Transmission expansion is a tariff issue, not only an engineering issue
TSEP rebuilds the grid for hydro evacuation, south-to-north transfer, KE integration, RE integration, automation and voltage support. The key economic issue is timing and cost allocation: if transmission is built for strategic public-good assets or delayed generation, consumer tariffs can carry fixed transmission costs before the associated demand or energy benefit materializes.

Capital Cost Estimation
From plant fixed costs to PKR/kWh capacity burden
The capital-cost engine has two linked layers. The first is the annual fixed-cost pool: existing fleet CPP plus annualized new IGCEP generation and TSEP costs. The second is the denominator: recoverable billed kWh. The same fixed rupee obligation becomes more expensive per kWh when the denominator weakens.

Capital recovery formula
Annualized capital recovery = CAPEX × CRF\n\nCRF = [r × (1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n − 1]\n\nCapacity cost per kWh = annual fixed obligation / recoverable billed kWh
Interpretation: WACC, tenor, FX and indexation determine annualized cost; recoverable units determine whether the same cost is affordable.

Tariff Model
Final tariff in PKR/kWh and US cents/kWh with losses, margin, inflation and FX depreciation
This tab recomputes the final tariff using live assumptions: distribution margin starts at the selected PKR/kWh base and escalates with the inflation slider; PKR/USD begins at 278 and depreciates with the FX slider; losses are grossed up at the selected loss rate. The default state is 7.0% inflation, 5.0% PKR depreciation and 20% losses. The final tariff is therefore not simply the earlier after-tax tariff field in the CSV.
Tariff formula used in this dashboard
DistributionMargin_t = 5 × (1 + 7%)^t\nFX_t = 278 × (1 + 5.0%)^t\nPreLossTariff_t = CapacityCost_t + VariableCost_t + T&D/SystemCost_t + DistributionMargin_t\nFinalTariffPKR_t = PreLossTariff_t ÷ (1 − LossRate)\nFinalTariffUS¢_t = FinalTariffPKR_t ÷ FX_t × 100

Important interpretation: Under the default 5.0% annual depreciation path, US cent tariffs may not rise as sharply as PKR tariffs.
