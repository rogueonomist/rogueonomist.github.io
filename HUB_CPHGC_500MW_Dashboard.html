The Hub Case: Anchoring a 500 MW Secondary Aluminium Cluster at CPHGC

China Power Hub Generation Company runs at 6.13% of its dependable capacity. A single co-located 500 MW off-taker cuts its own capacity cost by 84.7% — and, through the same mechanism illustrated at Port Qasim, begins compressing the tariff every consumer on the southern pool pays.

Section 01

CPHGC at Hub — an efficient plant with almost no customer

Why underutilisation, not inefficiency, is the story

CPHGC is not an expensive plant to run — it is an efficient plant whose enormous fixed capacity charge is divided across almost no output. The same PKR 124.46 billion, divided by 9,307.50 GWh instead of 671.77 GWh, turns a PKR 185.27/kWh asset into a PKR 13.37/kWh asset — a 92.8% collapse with no change to the debt schedule, ROE, or O&M contract. The plant does not need to get cheaper. It needs a customer.

The core mechanism
Pakistan already pays PKR 124.46 billion a year for CPHGC, whether it generates 671.77 GWh or 4,394.77 GWh. A 500 MW off-taker creates no new fiscal liability — the liability is already fully committed under a take-or-pay contract. What it creates is a buyer for capacity the country has already bought and is not using, cutting the plant's own per-unit cost by 84.7% — from PKR 185.27/kWh to PKR 28.32/kWh — using headroom the plant has today, with roughly 660 MW of further headroom left for a Phase 2 allocation.

Why this also means lower tariffs across the board — the Port Qasim parallel

The identical mechanism applies wherever southern surplus capacity is offtaken. Port Qasim (PQEPCL) illustrates it starting from a less extreme baseline than CPHGC — and shows the effect compounding across the three-plant pool even before Port Qasim itself is touched.

One 500 MW allocation at Hub — without touching Port Qasim or Lucky Electric — cuts the combined weighted capacity cost of the southern fleet by 45.8%. The full-headroom scenario across all three plants takes this to PKR 13.08/kWh; Hub alone gets roughly a third of the way there.

The caveat that matters
The PKR 68.75 → 37.24/kWh compression is real within the three-plant pool's own accounting. Whether it reaches Pakistan's general DISCO consumer tariff depends on a regulatory design choice that does not yet exist: the fixed-cost contribution must be explicitly credited into the national capacity-payment pool CPPA-G settles against — the project's policy work calls this the Incremental Load Capacity Contribution (ILCC). Without a NEPRA-embedded settlement rule requiring this crediting, the benefit can remain with the generator rather than reaching all consumers. Scale honesty also matters: national capacity payments run to roughly PKR 2.4–2.6 trillion/year against 100+ TWh of national sales — so this one transaction's PKR 18.6–29.8bn/year contribution is large for CPHGC specifically (15–24% of its own payment) but a modest ≈0.7–1.2% of the national pool. The case for a phased corridor, not a single allocation, follows directly from this.

Why 500 MW isn't a single furnace

Secondary aluminium's 500–1,000 kWh/tonne intensity, applied mechanically to 3,723 GWh, implies an output ceiling of 3.7–7.4 million tonnes/year — larger than most countries' entire annual secondary aluminium output. That ceiling is not achievable; scrap supply and sorting are the binding constraint, not electricity. The realistic 0.6–1.6 Mt/year range is only achievable as a multi-line cluster — remelting/alloying, casting, rolling/extrusion, cable and profile fabrication — not one furnace.

Gadani, 50 km along the coast, is a genuine but partial feedstock advantage: it generates 1.0–1.5 Mt/year of scrap, but overwhelmingly steel scrap. Aluminium content exists (fittings, wiring, superstructure) but at far lower, unquantified volume — a useful complementary source, not a primary one, unlike its role for EAF steel.

Summary
A 500 MW secondary-aluminium anchor at CPHGC lifts the plant's capacity factor from 6.13% to 40.13%, cuts its capacity cost by 84.7%, and funds 15–24% of its annual capacity payment from revenue that does not exist today — while compressing the combined southern three-plant capacity cost by 45.8%, illustrated identically at Port Qasim. It is bankable as a first tranche, not the full story: CPHGC alone retains ≈660 MW of further headroom, and Port Qasim and Lucky Electric remain almost untouched. Hub is Phase 1 of a corridor, not its destination.
