This dashboard quantifies the annualized capacity cost of each incremental MW-addition tranche — expressed in both US$/kW and PKR/kWh — across the full 10-year IGCEP horizon and for the life of each asset class.

Exhibit 3 — From CAPEX to PKR/kWh: The Capacity Cost Transmission Mechanism
Key Analytical Finding
The IGCEP's additional 21,141 MW of net new capacity, when annualized over asset lives and spread across the generation volumes these plants produce, adds an estimated PKR 8–12/kWh of capacity cost attributable to new additions alone. However, this cost is partially offset by the retirement of expensive RFO and legacy gas plants, whose variable fuel costs are eliminated. The net impact on the consumer tariff's capacity component depends critically on (a) how many kWh the new plants actually generate (load factor), (b) whether retirements reduce the existing CPP burden, and (c) exchange rate at time of dollar-denominated capacity payments.

Formula: Capacity Cost per kWh

Step 1 — Annual Capital Recovery (ACR) = CAPEX × CRF
CRF = WACC / [1−(1+WACC)^−N] —
Step 2 — Annual Generation (G) = MW × CF × 8,760 hrs
Step 3 — Capacity cost per kWh = ACR ÷ G
Convert to PKR × PKR/USD rate
Note: O&M is excluded — pure capital charge

Critical Insight — The Load Factor Problem
Pakistan's system load factor has declined from ~70% historically to 57% in FY2024. As solar PV penetrates (unidirectional — reduces midday consumption but not evening peak), the load factor falls further. This is structurally dangerous for capacity cost recovery: if new plants deliver fewer kWh than their design capacity factor, the PKR/kWh capacity cost rises. A 5% decline in effective capacity factor (22.8% → 17.8% for solar, if curtailment increases) raises solar's capacity cost from PKR 7.5 to PKR 9.6/kWh — a 28% deterioration. IGCEP's Low DSM scenario, which projects load factor recovery to ~70% by 2035, is therefore a necessary companion to the new investment plan, not optional.

Policy Conclusion
Pakistan is transitioning from a high-fuel, low-capital-cost generation fleet (RLNG, RFO) to a low-fuel, high-capital-cost fleet (hydro, solar, nuclear). This is a correct structural direction: fuel cost elimination de-risks consumers from commodity price shocks. But the transition imposes a front-loaded capital burden: PKR 7–17/kWh of capacity cost on new additions (depending on technology and exchange rate), compared to PKR 4–9/kWh variable fuel cost for RLNG/coal that it replaces. The financing structure — whether sovereign-backed, CPPA-G guaranteed, or CTBCM bilateral — determines who bears this capital risk and when. NCGCL's CTBCM de-risk products (CIG, CPFG, CPBG) are directly relevant to managing the imbalance volatility that arises during this transition period.
