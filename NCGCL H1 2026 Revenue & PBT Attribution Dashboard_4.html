01Headline Reconciliation

The Revenue Miss Is Real: What It Cost Us at PBT Is Not

Revenue came in 13.0% below Budget 2026. Profit Before Tax came in only 4.9% below it. That gap between the two numbers is the entire story of H1, and it did not happen by accident. We reconcile Budget PBT to Actual PBT below, to the rupee, across three moving parts: what the market took from us, and what discipline gave back.

PKR, H1 2026 (Jan–Jun) · Budget → Actual
Source: NCGCL Budget 2026 monthly P&L; NCGCL Management Accounts as at June 30, 2026
The revenue shortfall (−PKR 69.4M) is not a guarantee-business problem. It is almost entirely a capital-markets problem: return on our own balance sheet, not fee income from the guarantee book, which stays immaterial in rupee terms at this stage of our scale-up (Section 2).
Cost discipline (+PKR 55.1M combined) is not a footnote. It is the reason our margin held. We ran administrative and finance cost well under Budget, absorbed 79% of the revenue miss, and closed H1 with a PBT margin (60.0%) wider than the one we budgeted (54.9%).
02Revenue Decomposition

This Is Not a Guarantee-Fee Problem: It Is a Balance-Sheet-Return Problem

At our current scale, revenue is still overwhelmingly a function of what we earn on our own paid-up capital, not what we earn from guarantees; the fee book is still building. So when revenue misses, the honest question is never "what happened to guarantee fees." It is "what happened to the investment portfolio." That is the only question worth the Board's time in this section.

03Market Context

We Did Not Misjudge the Market: The Market Moved on Us

The mechanism is simple: the Hormuz Strait crisis (Feb 28 – Apr 8, 2026) repriced Pakistan's short end sharply higher through Q1–Q2, and rising benchmark yields mean mark-to-market losses on an existing bond book. That is the opposite of the gently-declining-rate world Budget 2026 was underwritten on. We built our numbers for one rate regime and got another.

04Attribution Analysis

Where the PKR 59.4M Went, and Where It Didn't

Two effects, and only two, explain the miss: we deployed a smaller capital base than planned, because we borrowed less than planned; and the rate environment moved against an existing book faster than we could reprice it. Strip out both, and what is left over from active management's own decisions (our alpha) is close to nil. That distinction matters more than the headline number.

We built Budget 2026 for a gently declining-rate world, consistent with SBP's 1,150 bps easing cycle through late 2025. The Hormuz shock reversed that path within weeks. That is the actual price of the crisis to this desk, and it is small.

06Efficiency Metric · Methodology

Why We Net Off the Cost of Funds Before We Call Ourselves Efficient

Gross revenue is not a management output. It is a function of how much balance sheet we deploy and what the market happens to charge for money that period. Divide admin cost by gross revenue and we are grading Treasury's leverage decisions, not how tightly we run the institution. So we don't. We net the cost of funds out first, the same way SBP, the IMF's Financial Soundness Indicators, and every bank or NBFI efficiency disclosure does it. That is the only version of this ratio that actually measures us.

Formula
The convention this dashboard applies
Cost-to-Income (Adjusted)  =  Administrative Expenses  ÷  ( Total Revenue − Finance Cost )
BasisActualBudgetRead
① Admin ÷ Gross Revenue (naive)24.31%24.12%Looks worse

③ Admin ÷ (Revenue − Finance Cost) (adjusted, correct)28.82%30.53%Genuinely better, −1.71 pts

All three lines above are arithmetically correct. Only the third answers the question the Board is actually asking (are we run efficiently) independent of how much we chose to borrow. That is the number we report as cost-to-income everywhere else in this pack.

Why Netting Off Finance Cost Is the Right Convention
Why not grossFinance cost is the price we pay for our raw material (money), not a discretionary line like rent or salaries. It moves with market rates and Treasury's leverage calls, not with how well we run operations. Divide admin cost by gross revenue and a pure financing decision (how much to borrow, at what rate) ends up grading our operating discipline. That's not a ratio; that's an accident.
The proof, in our numbersHere is the proof, in our own data: on the naive gross-revenue basis, our admin efficiency looks marginally worse than Budget, 24.31% against 24.12%, even though admin cost actually fell 12.4%. It looks worse only because gross revenue fell on lower leverage and rate volatility. Net the cost of funds out on both sides and the true picture flips: cost-to-income improves by 1.71 points. We report it this way because that's the version that's actually true.

Management View · H2 2026 Outlook

This Was a Timing Gap, Not a Structural Miss, and We Expect to Close It in H2.

Every piece of the H1 shortfall traces back to one thing: an external rate shock, not a lapse in portfolio judgment or operating discipline. Guarantee income doesn't move the current-scale P&L either way. The capital-base effect is the cost of a conservative borrowing stance during real rate volatility, not a missed opportunity, and we would make the same call again. Net trading alpha across the full six months came in at −3 bps, against a 168 bps swing in the benchmark; that is what disciplined risk management costs, and it is cheap. Investment income was already back above Budget in May and June as the environment stabilized. On an unchanged capital and borrowing plan for H2, we expect to close most of the residual timing gap by year-end, and we expect to keep the cost discipline, because that part was never about the crisis. That was just better management.
