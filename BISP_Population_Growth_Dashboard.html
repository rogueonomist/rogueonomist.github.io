Does BISP disbursement intensity relate to population growth?

One-line summary: districts with the highest share of BISP beneficiaries grew total population more slowly (≈2.2%/yr vs ≈3.0%/yr) and have a larger 2023 share of young children (34.0% vs 30.9% under age 10) — suggestive of domestic out-migration rather than lower fertility. But the best available true 2017–2023 child-growth measure (division-level) doesn't robustly confirm this once poverty is controlled for, so treat the migration explanation as plausible, not proven. See Birth-rate proxy, True 2017-23 growth and Conclusion.

01 · Data & methodology

How the dataset was built

1
Beneficiaries & populationPulled district-level BISP beneficiary counts from the workbook's Summary sheet. The workbook's own population figures were unreliable for ~1-in-6 matched districts (partial tehsil-level joins), so population was replaced with the census-sourced figure below for every district, for one consistent denominator.
2
DisbursementAggregated tehsil-level "Amount Deposited" figures (Jan–Mar 2023 quarter, Sheet1) up to the district level — 582 tehsil rows rolled into 131 district totals.
3
Population growthExtracted the official 2017 and 2023 populations and the published "2017–2023 average annual growth rate" from the Census-2023 district tables (Punjab, Sindh, KP, Balochistan, Islamabad PDFs).
4
Name matchingReconciled post-2017 district splits and naming variants: Chitral → Upper/Lower Chitral (aggregated), Kohistan → Kolai Palas / Lower / Upper Kohistan (aggregated), Kambar Shahdadkot, Tando Allahyar, Malakand Protected Area. Where districts were split, a population-weighted compound annual growth rate was recomputed from summed 2017/2023 populations.
5
ExclusionsAJK and Gilgit-Baltistan districts excluded (no census growth-rate tables available in source files). Ghanche and Lehri excluded (no matching census district). Final sample: 129 districts across Punjab, Sindh, KP, Balochistan and Islamabad.

06 · Birth-rate proxy (cross-sectional)

Does child population tell a different story than total growth?

When this section was first built, only Census-2023 Table 5 (population by age band) was available, with no matching 2017 breakdown — so this started as a cross-sectional fertility proxy: the 2023 share of population aged under 10 (and under 5 / under 15), rather than a literal growth rate. Young children rarely migrate independently, so this share is driven mostly by recent births rather than adult labour migration — a cleaner read on birth rates than total population growth, which bundles fertility, mortality and migration together. 2017 census age-band data has since been located and is analysed in Section 07 — read both sections together, since the true longitudinal measure tells a more cautious story than this cross-sectional one.

Reading the regression split: for under-10 and under-15 shares, the %Beneficiaries effect survives controlling for poverty score — there's a residual BISP-associated signal beyond what poverty alone explains. For under-5 share (the narrowest, most recent window), the effect is fully absorbed by poverty score — once you know how poor a district is, BISP intensity adds nothing further to predicting very recent births. Poverty score is the dominant predictor throughout (R²=0.70–0.80, far higher than the growth-rate model's R²=0.25), which is expected since BISP eligibility itself is poverty-scored.

07 · True 2017–2023 child growth (division-level)

A genuine before/after comparison — at coarser geography

Census-2017 age-band tables (Table 5) have since been located for all five regions. They give a real 2017 Under-10 population to compare against 2023 — but only at division level (Punjab's 9 divisions, Sindh's 6, KP's 7, Balochistan's 6), not district level, and KP's 2017 file excludes the seven districts that were still FATA agencies in 2017 (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Kurram, Orakzai, North & South Waziristan — merged into KP only in 2018). The division→district mapping was verified against the 2017 provincial population totals before use. Each district inherits its division's 2017–2023 Under-10 compound annual growth rate; 121 of 128 districts retain a value.

Why this tempers the Section 06 finding: the cross-sectional under-10 share (district-level, n=128, r=+0.43) and the true under-10 growth rate (division-level, n=121, r=−0.23) disagree on direction. A district's child share is a stock measure shaped by decades of fertility history — high-poverty districts simply have young population pyramids, BISP-targeted or not. The growth rate is a flow measure over just six years, computed at much coarser geography, and not robust to controlling for poverty. The honest read: the district-level cross-sectional pattern is real and worth noting, but it should not be read as strong evidence about how BISP districts' birth rates are currently trending — the best available longitudinal evidence is weak, noisy, and not poverty-independent.

08 · Conclusion

What this does — and doesn't — show

Finding
Three outcome variables, three different pictures — and the honest synthesis is more cautious than any single one. Total population growth is negatively correlated with BISP intensity (r ≈ −0.27, p < 0.01): high-BISP districts grew ~2.2%/yr vs. ~3.0%/yr for the rest. The 2023 cross-sectional under-10 child share — a fine-grained, district-level fertility proxy — is positively correlated (r ≈ +0.43, p < 0.0001): high-BISP districts have 34.0% of their population under 10 vs. 30.9% for the rest. But the true 2017–2023 under-10 growth rate — a genuine before/after comparison, though only available at coarser division level — comes back negative and weak (r ≈ −0.23, p = 0.01) and loses significance entirely once poverty score is controlled for (p = 0.64). The domestic-migration hypothesis remains plausible and is not contradicted by the data, but the strongest, most defensible piece of evidence (the true growth rate) does not robustly support it either way once granularity and poverty are accounted for.

Why none of this is a causal BISP effect: BISP targeting is itself based on a Multidimensional Poverty Index score — it deliberately concentrates in poor, rural districts. Those same districts (concentrated in interior Sindh, South Punjab and Balochistan in this sample) plausibly differ in baseline fertility, migration patterns, urbanization and female labour-force participation for reasons unrelated to BISP, none of which are in this dataset. The regression controls (poverty score, population size, province) show poverty score doing most of the explanatory work in every model — for the cross-sectional child share (R²=0.70–0.80, BISP intensity adds a smaller but still-significant residual effect) and especially for the true growth rate (R²=0.37, BISP intensity's effect disappears once poverty is in the model).

Data-quality caveats: disbursement reflects a single quarter (Jan–Mar 2023) while total-growth and division-growth figures reflect six-year census averages (2017–2023) — different reference periods. Table 5's own district totals differ from Table 1's by up to ~12% for two districts (Quetta, Kohistan), handled by using each table's internal denominator throughout. The true 2017–2023 growth rate is only available at division level (28 divisions covering 121 of 128 districts) — the seven districts that were still FATA agencies in 2017 (Bajaur, Mohmand, Khyber, Kurram, Orakzai, North & South Waziristan) have no 2017 comparison point and are excluded from that specific metric, though they retain the cross-sectional 2023 share. A handful of small, high-volatility Balochistan districts pull on the "rest" group's total-growth average and weaken significance under trimming (p≈0.07). AJK and Gilgit-Baltistan are excluded entirely for lack of matching census data; Islamabad is excluded from both child-population metrics (no Table 5 file available for it in either year).

Recommended next step: the migration hypothesis is testable directly if district-level net migration, commuting, or remittance data becomes available — that would separate "fewer people staying" from "fewer people being born" far more confidently than these proxies can. A genuinely district-level 2017 age-band table, if one exists, would also let the true growth-rate analysis run at the same resolution as the cross-sectional one rather than being diluted to 28 divisions. Failing either, urbanization rate and female literacy remain the most useful additional controls.
