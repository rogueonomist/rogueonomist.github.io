Pakistan's grid peak occurs in the small hours of a hot summer night — not because of industry, but because 160+ million fans and 14 million air conditioners are still running at midnight when rooftop solar is dark and heat has not yet relented. The cooling load is now a structural power-sector planning variable, not a weather footnote.

Planning insight: The IGCEP 2025-35 projects national system peak rising from 26,913 MW (FY2024) to 35,521 MW (FY2035) under Low BAU — a 2.6% CAGR. The cooling model shows that without demand-side intervention, the cooling peak alone could account for 10.7 GW (base) to 16.4 GW (high-heat) of that total by FY2030-31. Appliance standards, inverter AC penetration, and time-of-use tariffs are therefore generation capacity policy, not just consumer protection.

Hourly Demand Anatomy — Summer vs. Winter Profiles
The shift in Pakistan's hourly demand profile is the most direct fingerprint of the cooling-BTM solar interaction. In summer, BTM solar creates a midday trough (Hour 12) that can be 4,000–6,000 MW below the midnight peak (Hour 23). In winter, the load curve is flatter and peaks in the early evening (Hour 17–19). Each one-million-unit increase in AC stock deepens the evening-to-midnight ramp.

The late-night peak structure: In every summer month (May–Sep), the monthly-average demand is highest at Hour 23 or Hour 00. This is not an anomaly — it reflects that air conditioners run continuously through hot nights while BTM solar is absent. The RLNG-fired thermal fleet bears the brunt of this late-night peak (providing ~3,950 MW at the Jul-25 peak of 21,238 MW).

Fan Energy vs AC Energy Decomposition
TWh annually · ACs overtake fans as dominant energy user by mid-2020s

The structural shift — ACs overtaking fans: In FY2013-14, fans provided 55% of cooling energy (9.1 TWh) vs ACs at 45% (7.4 TWh). By FY2024-25 the ratio has inverted: ACs now drive 55% of cooling energy (13.9 TWh) and 50.7% of cooling peak. By FY2030-31, ACs will account for 60.9% of cooling energy. This is why minimum performance standards for ACs — particularly inverter technology adoption — are the highest-leverage single intervention in Pakistan's cooling demand trajectory.

System Peak Demand Forecast — IGCEP 2025-35 vs Cooling Model
The IGCEP 2025-35 projects national system peak rising from 26,913 MW (FY2024) to 35,521 MW (FY2035) under Low BAU and 43,069 MW under High growth — a 2.6%–4.4% CAGR. The cooling model shows that the FY2035 cooling peak under base assumptions is 10.7 GW, rising to 16.4 GW under high-heat/faster-AC adoption. The central question for planners: how much of the IGCEP peak growth is actually cooling growth that could be avoided through demand-side intervention?

The 11,698 MW summer-winter gap (FY2024, IGCEP-stated): The IGCEP itself notes that Pakistan's system peak has a seasonal spread of approximately 11,698 MW between summer and winter — with base load around 7,000 MW. The cooling model attributes essentially all of this seasonal swing to the fan and AC stock. This means that the ~11.7 GW of summer peaking capacity built, contracted, or maintained for cooling load must be paid for through capacity charges even when sitting idle in winter — a direct driver of the circular debt mechanism.

Policy Scenarios & Demand-Side Intervention
Three scenarios bound the planning space. The base case represents current trends with no structural change. The high-heat/faster-AC scenario reflects accelerating climate stress and income-driven AC adoption. The efficiency + demand response scenario captures a credible combination of minimum performance standards, inverter AC mandates, fan efficiency programs, time-of-use tariffs, and demand response programmes. The spread between scenarios — approximately 8 GW in FY2024-25, reaching ~10 GW by FY2030-31 — is the policy prize.

Inverter AC standards
Highest-leverage single intervention. Each additional million inverter ACs (vs non-inverter) saves ~100–140 MW at peak. Minimum Energy Performance Standards (MEPS) must mandate seasonal EER benchmarks.
Time-of-use tariffs
TOU pricing for domestic consumers should target 21:00–01:00 peak window — not midday. This is counter-intuitive because solar peaks at noon, but the cooling peak (and thermal cost) peaks at midnight.
Fan replacement programmes
Pakistan has ~160M fans at an average ~75–80W. Moving the average to 55W (through BLDC/EC fan standards) saves ~3,200–4,000 MW at peak nationally. Politically feasible, technically straightforward.

Capacity cost of inaction: Every 1 GW of cooling peak avoided through efficiency or demand response saves approximately PKR 8–12 billion annually in capacity payments (at ~PKR 8–12/kWh capacity charge equivalent for new peakers). The efficiency + DR scenario at FY2030-31 avoids ~4 GW vs the base case, implying PKR 32–48 billion in annual capacity cost savings — before the transmission and distribution cost reductions are counted.
