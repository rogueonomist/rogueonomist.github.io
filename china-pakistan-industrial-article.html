China–Pakistan Economic Corridor · Industrial Analysis · Updated May 2026

The Architecture of Dependence: How China Rewired Pakistan's Industrial Economy

A decade of CPEC has produced $25 billion in completed projects, 9,504 MW of new power capacity, and a mobile phone industry running almost entirely on Chinese IP. The numbers are impressive. So is the structural exposure they create.

Ammar H. Khan  ·  May 2026  ·  Data updated through 10M FY26 (SBP) and full-year 2025 (PTA, PAMA, PakWheels)
Exhibit 1 — The Capital Ledger

China Became Pakistan's Banker Before It Became Its Builder

Pakistan has attracted, on average, roughly $1.8–2.5 billion in net foreign direct investment per fiscal year over the past decade — modest against a 240-million-person economy and embarrassing against India's $71 billion FDI intake in 2024. What makes the composition remarkable is extreme concentration: in the peak CPEC years, China alone accounted for between 27 and 50 percent of Pakistan's total net FDI inflow. In FY25, the State Bank confirmed Chinese net FDI at $1.224 billion out of a national total of $2.46 billion — a 49.9 percent share, and a 90 percent surge from the $643 million recorded in FY24. A single bilateral partner provided half of Pakistan's total non-debt-creating foreign capital inflow.

The mechanism is straightforward. CPEC channelled Chinese state capital — through equity investment in IPPs, EXIM Bank concessional loans, and state-bank financing — into a country that had chronically underinvested in infrastructure for two decades. The architecture matters: the dominant mode is debt-creating project finance, not pure equity FDI. The recorded FDI numbers represent only the equity slice; the full CPEC financing stack, including loans, carried original project valuations exceeding $62 billion. In FY26, the picture has shifted: total net FDI in 10M FY26 (July–April 2026) declined 31 percent year-on-year to $1.41 billion amid governance concerns and policy uncertainty flagged by the SBP — yet China remained the largest single source with $739 million, accounting for over 52 percent of that diminished total. The dependence is structural, not cyclical.

The FY25 surge — 90 percent YoY growth in Chinese FDI — reflects the acceleration of hydropower projects under CPEC Phase II, particularly Suki Kinari (884 MW, COD September 2024) and associated balance-of-plant investment. FY26's overall FDI contraction, attributed by the SBP to governance concerns and policy uncertainty, has not dislodged China from its position as anchor investor: the bilateral relationship has a structural depth that cyclical investor hesitancy cannot easily disrupt.

Exhibit 2 — The Grid Transformation

Power: 9,504 MW Added, But the Structural Problem Remains

Pakistan in 2013 had a power deficit measured in thousands of megawatts. Load-shedding ran 12–18 hours daily in industrial zones. Chinese investment addressed the supply problem with speed: between 2015 and 2024, 14 generation projects under CPEC reached commercial operation, adding a combined 9,504 MW — roughly one-fifth of Pakistan's entire installed capacity today. A single HVDC transmission line, the 660 kV Matiari–Lahore interconnector, added 4,000 MW of evacuation capacity at a cost of $1.7 billion.

The portfolio is coal-heavy by design — over 70 percent of CPEC-added capacity is coal-fired. Renewable additions under CPEC proper were limited: 700 MW from one solar park and four wind farms accounts for under 8 percent of CPEC-added generation. This was not an oversight; coal plant technology was mature, financeable, and deliverable on an accelerated timeline. The cost consequence — locked-in capacity payments to Chinese IPPs in US dollars — now sits at the centre of Pakistan's circular debt crisis. But that is a problem of contract design, not of the investment itself.

The Solar Postscript — China's Market-Driven Energy Disruption

Beyond CPEC-financed projects, China's most disruptive energy contribution to Pakistan in 2024–25 was market-driven, not state-directed. Pakistan imported 17 GW of solar panels from China in 2024 alone — more than any other country on earth — at a cost of approximately $1.4 billion in H1 2024 alone. Net-metered rooftop solar reached 5.3 GW by April 2025, making solar the largest single electricity source in Pakistan at over 25% of generation. Chinese manufacturing overcapacity that collapsed global panel prices is reshaping Pakistan's energy mix from the bottom up, faster than any state-directed programme could. The next CPEC chapter — a "green corridor" announced in October 2024 — will bring large-scale solar into the CPEC framework formally.

Exhibit 3 — The Connectivity Layer

Highways: 677 km of Motorway, One Metro, One Airport

Infrastructure poverty has been Pakistan's most persistent economic drag. CPEC's programme — executed primarily by Chinese state construction firms — delivered the country's most significant road network expansion in a generation within a compressed five-year window. The M-5 Multan–Sukkur motorway, executed by China State Construction Engineering Corporation and completed two weeks ahead of schedule in 2019, slashed travel time between southern Punjab and upper Sindh from over seven hours to under three. The M-14 Hakla–D.I.Khan corridor connected the capital to Dera Ismail Khan, opening the western route for the first time to national freight networks. New Gwadar International Airport, inaugurated October 2024 by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and Premier Li Qiang, is the corridor's most visible recent completion.

Exhibit 4 — The Digital Stack

Telecom: Zong, Huawei, ZTE, and the 30-Million-Phone Factory

China's penetration of Pakistan's digital economy has three layers — network infrastructure, network operation, and device manufacturing. Together they have produced a structural dependency more complete than in any other sector.

Network Infrastructure. Huawei and ZTE supply the physical layer — base stations, switching equipment, fibre interconnects, and 5G-readiness upgrades — to every major Pakistani mobile operator. Zong's H1 2024 network modernisation added 400 new 4G sites and 1,400+ power upgrades on Huawei and ZTE hardware. As the PTA prepares for Pakistan's long-awaited 5G spectrum auction, Zong signed an MoU with ZTE in January 2026 to deliver affordable 5G smartphones bundled with data packages — tying the device supply chain directly to the network operator.

Network Operation. Zong — formally CMPak Ltd, a wholly-owned subsidiary of China Mobile Communications Corporation — has grown from a 2 percent market share at entry in 2008 to 54 million subscribers and 26 percent of Pakistan's mobile market. It is the second-largest operator. With the proposed Jazz-Ufone merger still under antitrust review, Zong could eventually move to the top position. A Chinese state-owned enterprise now carries one in four Pakistani mobile connections.

Exhibit 5 — The Automotive Disruption

Automobiles: Changan Is Now Pakistan's 4th Largest Brand

For fifty years, Pakistan's passenger vehicle market was a Japanese oligopoly. The entry of Chinese automakers — beginning incrementally and accelerating sharply after 2022 — represents the first genuine competitive disruption Pakistani consumers have experienced in a generation. By 2025, that disruption had become structural. Master Changan Motors sold over 65,000 units in 2025, making it the 4th largest automobile brand in Pakistan, jumping two positions in a single year and displacing Honda from the podium in certain months. Haval (Sazgar Engineering) sold 13,943 units of its SUV lineup, making it the dominant SUV brand by volume — outpacing Toyota's Fortuner and Hilux combined (9,547 units).

The total PAMA-reported market reached 166,476 units in 2025, a 33 percent recovery from 125,048 in 2024. The Japanese brands maintained aggregate dominance — Suzuki at roughly 44 percent, Toyota at 19 percent — but the structural question is what the market looks like inclusive of non-PAMA reporters. Changan (65,000 units), MG, Kia, and Proton all report outside PAMA. Adding these estimates, the total Pakistan automotive market in 2025 likely exceeded 240,000–250,000 units, with Chinese brands (Changan + Haval + MG + BAIC + Chery) accounting for an estimated 30–35 percent of the expanded total. Meanwhile, BYD began delivering imported EVs in Pakistan in March 2025 and announced local assembly from 2026.

The EV Frontier: BYD Arrives

In March 2025, BYD began delivering imported EVs in Pakistan — the world's largest EV maker making its first commercial foray into one of Asia's largest untapped markets. A few hundred cars exceeded internal targets by 30%, according to Reuters. BYD Pakistan then announced local assembly from 2026, initially from imported CKD kits with potential expansion into right-hand-drive export markets. The government cut EV charging tariffs by 45% in January 2025. Pakistan's EV and plug-in hybrid market, estimated at roughly 1,000 units in 2024, is projected by BYD's own Pakistan partner to grow 3–4 times in 2025. Chinese automakers are not just competing in Pakistan's ICE market — they are defining its EV market from day one.

Analysis

What a Decade of Chinese Capital Has Built — and What It Has Not

Aggregated across sectors, the Chinese contribution to Pakistan's industrial and digital economy over the past decade is difficult to minimise. 38 CPEC projects worth over $25 billion have reached completion. Power outages that ran 14 hours daily in 2013 are structurally reduced. 677 kilometres of motorway opened previously inaccessible corridors to economic activity. A mobile phone manufacturing sector that did not functionally exist in 2016 now produces 30 million units annually — 95 percent of domestic demand — on Chinese technology licenses and Chinese-designed production lines. 54 million Pakistanis access mobile services through a Chinese state-owned carrier. And Chinese automakers have, in two years, broken a fifty-year Japanese oligopoly — Changan alone sold 65,000 vehicles in 2025, more than Honda's entire Pakistani franchise.

What CPEC has not yet produced — and what a second decade must deliver if it is to justify the debt obligations it created — is a manufacturing export base. Pakistan's goods export basket has not structurally changed despite a decade of Chinese industrial partnerships. Assembly is not manufacturing: 90-plus percent of component inputs in Pakistan's mobile phone "factories" are imported from China. The distance from "assembled in Pakistan using Chinese kits" to "manufactured in Pakistan with Pakistani components and exported" is the distance CPEC Phase II must close. That the FDI surge in FY25 went overwhelmingly into power (hydel projects) and financial services rather than into export-platform industrialisation underscores how incomplete the transformation remains.

The Forcing Close
China built Pakistan's grid, paved its highways, wired its towers, stocked its phone shelves, and is now selling its electric cars on financing terms. By 10M FY26, China accounts for 52 cents of every dollar of net FDI Pakistan receives. The task for the next decade is to convert that infrastructure dependency into an export production platform — because without it, Pakistan is left with Chinese-financed assets, Chinese-denominated debt service, and a trade deficit with China that has widened from $7 billion in FY19 to over $16 billion by FY24. Infrastructure is a necessary condition for industrialisation. It is not sufficient. The second chapter of CPEC must be written by Pakistani industrial policy, not Chinese project finance.
