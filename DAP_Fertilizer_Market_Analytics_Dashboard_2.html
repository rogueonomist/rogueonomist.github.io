iCore Insight
DAP is a fertilizer Pakistan mostly buys from abroad, mainly used to plant wheat. This dashboard asks five simple questions and answers all of them with real numbers: How has the price moved over the years? Why do farmers pay more than the rest of the world pays? Do farmers buy less when it gets expensive? Does that answer change depending on the time of year? And if the government subsidizes the price, how much would that actually cost per extra bag sold? Short version: Pakistani farmers pay well above the world price, they barely cut back even when it's expensive — except at planting season, when price hikes bite noticeably harder.

What this covers
A full read of Pakistan's DAP fertilizer market: how local retail prices track international benchmarks, why local price sits well above parity, how demand responds to price, and what that means for the subsidy designs currently under discussion (Punjab's Rs 3,000/bag Rabi scheme; the federal Kissan Package).

iCore Insight
Since Pakistan imports most of its DAP, the local price mostly rises and falls together with the world price over time — that part is expected. What's notable is that the local price is always higher than the world price, by 48% on average. Some of that gap is completely normal (shipping it here, moving it around the country, the profit shops need to make to stay open). A small slice is government tax (5%, only since 2023). And part of the gap isn't a real cost at all — it's just a timing lag: when world prices jump suddenly, it takes local prices a few months to catch up, which makes the gap look bigger or smaller than it really is for a while.

iCore Insight
Fertilizer buying in Pakistan is driven almost entirely by the farming calendar, not by price. Farmers buy roughly four times as much DAP in October–November (wheat planting season) as they do in January–February — that's just when the crop needs it. Meanwhile, even though the price has risen roughly five-fold since 2015, the total amount farmers buy each year has stayed in roughly the same range, moving up or down for other reasons (bad harvests, import shortages) rather than because of price. This is the first clue that fertilizer isn't really optional for farmers — it's a "must-buy" tied to the growing season, not a discretionary purchase they can easily skip.

iCore Insight
"Elasticity" is just a technical name for one simple question: if the price goes up 10%, how much less do people actually buy? A number close to zero means people barely change their behavior at all — they need the product regardless of cost. Here, the numbers are consistently low: for every 10% price rise, farmers cut purchases by only about 6–8% — further confirmation that farmers don't have a real substitute for DAP. The more surprising finding: during the October–November planting rush, when everyone is buying at once, that price-sensitivity roughly doubles. Farmers pull back more sharply on a price spike exactly at the moment they're buying the most — and we specifically checked this wasn't just a fertilizer shortage pretending to look like a price effect. It held up.

Cross-checked against the DAP production/import/export/inventory balance sheet. Each point = one October or November, 2014–2025, plotted against that calendar month's own historical average.
Offtake anomaly vs. stock-cover anomaly, peak months
Bottom-left = supply-constrained · bottom-right = demand pullback (ample supply, buyers held back)
Key correction from earlier analysis: October 2022 sits at 23% of normal offtake with stock cover at 441% of normal — supply was ample and piling up, not scarce. That is clean evidence of a genuine price-driven demand pullback at the highest price level in the series, not rationing. By contrast, Oct/Nov-2023 and Nov-2024 show low offtake and low stock cover together — genuine supply shortages tied to the 2023–24 import/LC restriction period. Excluding only those four genuinely supply-constrained months (not all of 2022, as a coarser cut would) strengthens the peak elasticity estimate to −1.20 (p=0.014), up from an earlier, more conservative −1.11.
iCore Insight
There's a common worry in food-security circles: if farmers can't afford enough DAP, wheat output should fall — some estimates put the loss at around 1 million tons a year. We tested this directly. What we find is more complicated, and in the clearest recent test case, output moved opposite to what the DAP-shortage story predicts. Between 2016–17 and 2022–23, planting-season DAP purchases fell by nearly two-thirds as prices quadrupled — and wheat yield hit a record high in exactly the year DAP purchases were at their lowest. The one recent season where output really did fall hard (2024–25, −8.9%) saw DAP offtake rising, not falling; press and government reporting point to a smaller sown area, following a support-price cut, and poor winter rainfall — not a fertilizer shortfall. There is, however, a real link running the other direction: when wheat prices are high, farmers buy noticeably more DAP about three months later — farmer cash flow shapes fertilizer buying, alongside DAP's own price.

Two mechanisms, both plausible, pointing opposite directions in the lag structure: wheat-price growth ~3–4 months earlier is followed by significantly higher DAP-offtake growth (farmer income/terms-of-trade effect — consistent with the crop calendar: post-harvest cash in hand around Apr–Jul feeding into Oct–Dec planting decisions). Separately, DAP-offtake growth now is followed by significantly lower flour-price growth 5–9 months later (F≈2.1–2.3, p≈0.06–0.07) — a supply-response signal: more fertilizer applied at planting is followed by softer prices at the next harvest. The annual MSP series was tested as an alternative farmer-income proxy but is confounded — MSP was explicitly raised to compensate for the DAP price shock, so the two move together mechanically (r=−0.94 with same-season DAP offtake, n=5) and cannot cleanly separate an income effect from the cost shock itself.
Caveats before this goes into a formal deliverable: the core annual panel is n=9 — every coefficient there is suggestive, not confirmatory. DAP offtake is dispatch into the distribution channel, not confirmed on-farm application, and it is not split by crop (Oct–Dec DAP also covers potato, gram, oilseeds). Wheat flour price is a retail milled-product price, not a farmgate price. A precise "tons of wheat lost per ton of DAP under-applied" estimate needs an agronomic yield-response curve (on-station P-response trial data), which is a different exercise from this national time-series regression and hasn't been built here. 2023-24/2024-25 production figures are press/PBS-sourced, not from the project's own Agriculture Statistics file, and vintages disagree by 1–3M tons across sources.
iCore Insight
If the government makes DAP cheaper through a subsidy, a lot of that money will go to farmers who would have bought it anyway at full price — that's simply how subsidies work when people don't change their buying much (see the Elasticity tab). Our estimate: for every extra ton of fertilizer that gets bought specifically because of the subsidy — not counting what would have sold anyway — it costs the government somewhere between Rs 320,000 and Rs 590,000. The good news: Punjab's proposed Rs 20 billion budget looks large enough to cover the scheme even in the costlier scenarios. The open question we can't yet answer with the data available is whether the subsidy is reaching the right farmers — that would need more detailed records than currently exist.
