Global Crude & Products
How oil prices evolved through the Hormuz crisis
The closure of the Strait of Hormuz — through which ~20 mb/d of crude, condensate, and refined products normally transit — created the largest supply disruption in oil market history. Prior to the conflict, global markets were oversupplied and prices were falling. The shock reversed this entirely: Brent futures soared from $72/bbl before the war to nearly $128/bbl at the April 2 peak, while physical crude benchmarks disconnected sharply from futures, with Dubai crude touching $166/bbl. Product markets were even more severely disrupted, with middle distillate cracks reaching all-time highs.

Supply disruption scale: The IEA estimates Gulf crude production fell by at least 10 mb/d by mid-March, with overall global supply disrupted by 7.5–9.1 mb/d at its peak. Retail Fuel Prices
Pump prices — Pakistan weekly timeline & regional peer comparison
Pakistan's pump prices are determined fortnightly (shifted to weekly during the crisis) by OGRA using an Import Parity Price formula referenced to Arab Gulf Platts indices. As of May 23, 2026, petrol is Rs. 403.78/L and HSD is Rs. 402.78/L — representing a 55% increase over pre-war levels. From the April 3 peak of Rs. 458.41/L, petrol has moderated as crude prices partially retreated, but the PDL floor (Rs. 78/L) and restored GST (17%) prevent full pass-through of crude price declines.

Policy Analysis
Pakistan pump price vs. Arab Gulf import parity — three phases
The relationship between Pakistan's pump price and the international Arab Gulf Gasoline MOPS 92 benchmark reveals three distinct policy phases during the Hormuz crisis. The premium — the gap between Pakistan's pump price (in USD/L) and the import reference price — captures the tax and policy wedge. In Phase 1, Pakistan lagged the rising global price, absorbing costs. In Phase 2, the massive April 3 PDL hike overshot, pushing Pakistanis above international market rates for the first time. In Phase 3, international prices fell but Pakistan's pump price remained sticky, driven by the Rs. 78/L PDL floor and restored 17% GST.
Arab Gulf Gasoline MOPS 92 vs. Pakistan Petrol Pump Price & Tax/Policy Premium — Feb 26 to May 23, 2026
Phase 1 — Policy Lag (Mar 4–Apr 2)
Arab Gulf gasoline surged from $0.52 to $1.35/L while Pakistan stayed at $0.96–$1.16/L. The government absorbed $0.17–$0.30/L before passing it on, building a subsidy liability that crystallised on April 3. The premium compressed toward zero and briefly turned negative.
Phase 2 — Overshoot (Apr 3–16)
After the April 3 hike, Pakistan's pump price ($1.65/L) exceeded the Arab Gulf product price ($1.35/L) — the PDL hike to Rs. 161/L pushed Pakistanis above international market levels. A pure tax-on-top-of-crisis moment. Brief PDL reversal (Apr 5) brought limited relief.
Phase 3 — Sticky Premium (Apr 17–Now)
Arab Gulf gasoline has fallen to ~$0.85/L, but Pakistan pump price remains at $1.45/L — a ~$0.60/L premium driven entirely by the PDL (Rs. 78/L) and GST (17%). Prices are not symmetric: they rise faster than they fall. At current crude, import parity alone would support a pump price of ~Rs. 280–290/L.
The sticky premium in numbers: At current MOPS 92 of ~$0.85/L and PKR 278/$, the pure import parity price for petrol (CIF + port + distribution margins, no tax) is approximately Rs. 270–285/L. Pakistan's actual pump price of Rs. 403.78 implies a government tax take of Rs. 120–130/L — a premium of 42–48% over import cost. This is structurally locked in by the PDL Rs. 78/L (IMF-constrained) and 17% GST.

Key policy insight — asymmetric pass-through: When MOPS 92 rose from $0.52 to $1.35/L (+160%), Pakistan's pump price initially lagged then overshot. Now that MOPS has fallen from $1.35 to $0.85/L (−37%), the pump price has fallen from Rs. 458 to Rs. 403.78 (−12%). The asymmetry is structural: the PDL is fixed at Rs. 78/L regardless of crude, and GST at 17% amplifies any remaining base. For pump prices to return to pre-war levels (~Rs. 260), MOPS 92 would need to fall to ~$0.42/L — below pre-war levels — or the PDL would need to be cut to near zero. The IMF program makes the latter politically and fiscally infeasible.
Price Level Impact
Inflation transmission — how the fuel shock spread through Pakistan's economy
Pakistan's consumer price index returned to double digits (10.9% YoY) in April 2026 — for the first time in 21 months — driven by the cascading effect of the fuel price shock. The transmission works across multiple channels: direct transport costs, agricultural production costs, manufacturing inputs, power generation, and second-round wage effects. The weekly Sensitive Price Indicator (SPI) published by PBS captures real-time price changes in 51 essential items across 17 urban centres.

Diesel (HSD) Tax Note

In the April 3 hike, the government abolished the PDL on HSD — while raising it to Rs. 161/L on petrol — in response to diesel's extreme economic sensitivity for freight and agriculture. By May 23, HSD (Rs. 402.78/L) carries roughly Rs. 75–85/L in GST taxes (~19% of pump price), with PDL remaining at zero on HSD.

IMF Program Constraint

Pakistan's Extended Fund Facility with the IMF prohibits permanent fuel subsidies. The government must collect at least Rs. 1,070 billion/year in petroleum levies. This forced the April 3 PDL hike to Rs. 161/L — and means even "temporary" PDL cuts must be reversed. Pakistan's import bill surged from $300M/week pre-war to $800M/week, erasing two years of fiscal gains.

Motorcycle Subsidy Scheme

Following the April 3 shock, the PM introduced a Rs. 100/L motorcycle petrol subsidy (up to 20 litres per fill for 3 months) delivered via CNIC/biometric verification at designated pumps. This reduces the effective price for ~18 million motorcycle owners from Rs. 403/L to ~Rs. 303/L — targeted relief to lower-income daily commuters.
