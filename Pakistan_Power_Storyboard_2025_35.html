Pakistan's Capacity Cost Crisis:
Demand Fracture Meets Capacity Lock-In

A five-act storyboard tracing Pakistan's power sector from the demand side to the supply side and finally to the price signal consumers and regulators must confront: Rs./kWh of capacity cost. The central thesis — that behind-the-meter solar and batteries will hold grid demand near flat while IGCEP capacity commitments add Rs. 400–600 billion in new capacity payments — produces a structural elevation in capacity cost per unit of grid electricity that no tariff framework currently acknowledges.

Act I
The Fractured Demand Equation
Historically, total electricity demand and grid demand were nearly identical — the grid was the only source. The equation has broken. Behind-the-meter solar now supplies 30.4 TWh annually (21% of retail demand) from 26.8 GW of installed capacity. Batteries are accelerating the fracture into evening hours. Grid demand peaked at 154 TWh in FY2022 and has declined to 140.4 TWh (gross) despite 3% GDP growth. The divergence between total electricity demand and grid demand is structural — not cyclical.

Act II
The Capacity Lock-In: IGCEP Adds Anyway
While grid demand stagnates, Pakistan's Indicative Generation Capacity Expansion Plan 2025–35 schedules 21,537 MW of new capacity additions — dominated by large hydro (Dasu, Diamer Bhasha) and successive waves of RE. These are not discretionary investments; many carry sovereign construction commitments. The capacity is being added into an already over-built system. Pakistan's reserve margin already exceeds 60% of peak demand. Every new MW locked in means another Rs. that grid consumers must pay regardless of whether the energy is dispatched.

Act II Finding
By FY2034-35, total installed capacity will reach approximately 70,000–75,000 MW against peak demand of roughly 28,000–32,000 MW — an implicit reserve margin of 130–160%. Under the stagnant grid scenario, each MW of capacity is serving fewer kWh of grid demand with every passing year. The fixed cost obligation — the capacity purchase payment — does not diminish with dispatch. It accumulates. This is the structural source of Pakistan's circular debt: a cost that grows while the denominator that should absorb it stagnates.
Act III
The Cost Escalation: CPP Rising on Two Fronts
Existing fleet CPP is rising — held up by currency depreciation and CPI indexation — but is partly offset by declining interest as debt matures (Sahiwal exits by FY2034, Port Qasim by FY2038). New IGCEP projects add a second and growing layer. Debt service on new projects is front-loaded: in Year 1 of each cohort, interest alone accounts for 45–50% of total capacity payment. Under the 70-30 debt-equity structure and 13-year amortization, the FCY debt burden is directly sensitive to SOFR and PKR/USD — adjust both sliders above to see the impact.

Act III Finding
Existing fleet CPP rises modestly from Rs. 1,731B to Rs. 1,840B over the decade — a 6% increase driven by CPI and FX indexation, partly offset by debt maturities. New IGCEP projects add a steep second layer that grows with each successive commissioning cohort. The total CPP burden reaches Rs. 2,300–2,500B by FY2034-35 depending on macro assumptions. Every 100bps rise in SOFR adds approximately Rs. 18–24B to annual new project interest costs.
Act IV — The Headline
The Collision: Capacity Cost Per Unit of Grid Electricity
This is the central metric. Total CPP (Rs. Billion) divided by grid retail demand (TWh) gives the capacity cost per kWh that grid consumers implicitly bear. When CPP rises and demand grows in tandem, the per-unit cost is absorbed. When CPP rises while demand stagnates — as solar and batteries hold grid demand flat — the per-unit burden escalates sharply. This is the scissors effect: costs rising, denominator stagnating.

Act V — The Verdict
Policy Implications: What the Numbers Demand
The arithmetic is unambiguous. If grid demand stagnates — which the BTM solar and battery trajectory makes increasingly likely — Pakistan's capacity cost per kWh will reach Rs. 20–22 by FY2034-35, representing 40–45% of the retail bill before a single unit of energy is generated. Three interventions follow directly from this analysis.
Intervention 1
Renegotiate existing tariffs
Pakistan has already begun negotiating down LPI and capacity payments with major CPEC and Thar coal plants. This analysis quantifies the stakes: Rs. 1.8–1.9 trillion of existing CPP that must be compressed. Each 10% reduction in existing CPP saves Rs. 173–184B annually — enough to reduce tariffs by Rs. 1.5/kWh under base demand assumptions.
Intervention 2
Condition new capacity on demand absorption
The IGCEP pipeline adds 21,537 MW regardless of grid demand absorption. New contracts should carry demand-contingent capacity payments — lower fixed charges when utilisation falls below threshold. This shifts construction-period risk back to developers and aligns incentives with the grid's actual absorptive capacity. Hydro mega-projects (Dasu, Bhasha) require special treatment as sovereign assets.
Intervention 3
Grow the grid denominator
The fastest path to lower CPP/kWh is a larger denominator — more kWh sold through the grid. EV charging infrastructure, industrial electrification, and export-oriented manufacturing can grow grid demand without building new supply. A Rs. 1 rise in CPP/kWh can be neutralised by a 15 TWh increase in grid retail demand — roughly 13% above the FY2025 base. Policy should treat demand growth as a cost management instrument.
Closing Statement
The capacity cost crisis is not primarily a tariff problem, a debt problem, or even a planning problem. It is a demand problem. Every analysis of Pakistan's power sector that begins with the supply side and works forward to costs is working in the wrong direction. The relevant question is not how much capacity costs — it is how many kWh of grid demand exist to absorb that cost. By FY2034-35, on current trajectories, grid consumers will be paying Rs. 20–22/kWh in capacity charges alone, before energy costs, T&D losses, and distribution margin. That is the structural fact this analysis produces. It should inform every tariff petition, every renegotiation, and every new PPA signed.
