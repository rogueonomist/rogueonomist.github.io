What is happening right now
  • Households pay mostly through one bundled per-unit rate.
  • Fixed grid costs are hidden inside the energy charge.
  • There is weak incentive to move usage from 8pm to cheap solar hours.
  • Solar users reduce bills, but do not necessarily pay for backup grid readiness.
  • If V8 is applied literally to residential prosumers, the 50% sanctioned-load floor creates severe bill shock.
What we want to achieve
  • Split the bill into a fixed grid-readiness charge and a variable energy charge.
  • Show consumers when electricity is cheap, normal, and expensive.
  • Protect lifeline and middle-income consumers through a phased transition.
  • Make batteries and smart demand management valuable because they cut evening peak.
  • Keep solar users connected to the grid, but make their network contribution fair.
The whole reform in one sentence: start with a small, explainable fixed charge now; then move to smart-meter billing where consumers who reduce peak demand pay less.
The bill structure
The tariff should send two separate signals: grid readiness and time of use.
Simple formula
Bill = Fixed grid charge + Time-of-use energy charge
Meaning: the fixed charge pays for the grid and capacity kept available. The energy charge pays for actual electricity consumed, with lower rates when the system is cheap and higher rates during evening peak.

Three proposals clarified
These are three different policy choices — not three minor variations.

V8 as-designed

What it suggests: apply the official V8 domestic structure literally: Rs 10,085/kW-month capacity charge, ToU energy rates, and a minimum billing demand of 50% of sanctioned load.

Policy reading: technically cost-reflective, but too sharp for residential rollout and especially punitive for solar / battery consumers.

Proposed Phase‑1

What it suggests: begin with a simple transitional charge before AMI. For prosumers, charge Rs 400/kW sanctioned load per month. A 10 kW user pays Rs 4,000/month as the fixed grid charge.

Policy reading: politically viable bridge. It starts grid-cost recovery without creating an overnight backlash.

Proposed AMI design

What it suggests: once smart meters are available, use actual measured peak demand, but change the minimum floor by consumer type: 50% grid-only, 35% solar-only, 20% solar+BESS, 40% BESS-only.

Policy reading: the desired end-state. It rewards genuine peak reduction rather than treating every household the same.

Bill shock under V8 vs smoother proposed path
The message to policymakers
  • Do not sell this as a tariff increase. Sell it as a better price signal.
  • Protect low-use households first. Their bills should not be the reform battlefield.
  • Do not punish solar users overnight. Move from Rs 20/kWh export credit to a lower but explainable premium.
  • Make peak reduction the core incentive. A battery is valuable because it lowers MDI, not because it trades a few kWh.

Rollout
A three-step path that is easier to explain and harder to derail.

1. Start softly

Protect lifeline consumers. Introduce a small Phase‑1 fixed charge for prosumers and high-use households. Communicate that this is a grid-readiness charge, not a penalty.

2. Meter properly

Install AMI for high-use, solar, and battery consumers first. Start billing on actual measured peak demand rather than rough sanctioned-load proxies.

3. Reward behaviour

Apply differentiated floors once AMI is available: 50% grid-only, 35% solar-only, 20% solar+BESS, 40% BESS-only. Consumers who reduce evening peak pay less.

Final policy ask: approve Phase‑1 as the bridge, approve AMI floors as the end-state, and avoid launching the unmodified V8 50% floor on residential prosumers.
