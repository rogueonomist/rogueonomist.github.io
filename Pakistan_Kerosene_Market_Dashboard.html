Section 1 · Demand
Structural decline with an FY2024-25 price-driven rebound
Pakistan's kerosene market consumed 65,964 tonnes in FY2024-25 — a +15.85% rebound after three consecutive years of contraction. Yet the five-year ACGR remains −5.8%, confirming a structural displacement by rural electrification and LPG. The rebound is primarily price-driven: average sale prices fell ~Rs 28/L YoY as global crude softened, pulling demand back from substitute fuels at the margin. At typical demand elasticities of −0.3 to −0.5, that price compression predicts a 12–18% volume response — closely matching the observed +15.85%.

Why did consumption rebound? The proximate mechanism is a price effect. Ex-refinery prices tracked Brent crude downward throughout FY2024-25, compressing the average fixed sale price from approximately Rs 195/L (FY2023-24) to approximately Rs 167/L — a Rs 28/L decline. At demand elasticities of −0.3 to −0.5 (typical for basic energy fuels), this predicts a 12–18% volume response, closely matching the observed +15.85%. The Mar–Apr 2025 Customs Duty escalation partially offset further gains, stabilising consumer prices near Rs 165–170/L despite continued ex-refinery softening.

Section 2 · Pricing
Fortnightly price notifications — FY2023-24 versus FY2024-25, full series
Kerosene prices are notified fortnightly by DG(Oil), tracking ex-refinery prices (which follow Brent crude) plus a thin, largely fixed margin structure. FY2023-24 saw a dramatic spike to Rs 244.81/L in September 2023 before a sustained retreat. FY2024-25 opened 6% lower than the previous year's opening and fell further to a trough of Rs 154.90/L in October 2024 — before a Q4 Customs Duty escalation stabilised consumer prices in the Rs 165–170/L range despite continued ex-refinery softening.

Q4 FY2024-25 fiscal inflection: Customs/Excise Duty on kerosene was raised from Rs 0.05/L to Rs 10.96/L on 16 March 2025 (a 219× increase) and further to Rs 18.95/L on 16 April 2025. This absorbed approximately Rs 19/L of ex-refinery price relief that would otherwise have passed through to consumers, effectively pegging retail prices near Rs 168–170/L while global crude traded below US$70/bbl. The move represents the first meaningful fiscal tightening on kerosene — a product previously treated as an untouchable social fuel — in several years.

Section 3 · Mechanism
How the kerosene price is built — five components and a deliberate tax concession
The fixed sale price of kerosene is constructed from five components: (1) ex-refinery price, tracking Brent crude and representing 83–95% of the total; (2) Customs/Excise Duty, nominal until Mar 2025, then escalated sharply; (3) Petroleum Development Levy — fixed at Rs 1.58/L throughout (versus Rs 60–78/L for petrol); (4) OMC Distribution Margin, covering storage, transport, and handling at Rs 7–9/L; and (5) Dealer's Commission — waived at Rs 0.00 for kerosene. GST is fully exempt. The combined tax and margin burden for kerosene was Rs 27.98/L in June 2025 versus Rs 116.45/L for petrol — a Rs 88.47/L gap worth approximately PKR 7 billion per year in foregone revenue.

Fiscal cost of the kerosene concession: At Rs 88.47/L lower tax burden than petrol, and approximately 80 million litres consumed annually (65,964 T ÷ ~0.82 kg/L density), revenue foregone relative to petrol's fiscal treatment is approximately PKR 7 billion per year. The April 2025 Customs Duty hike (Rs 0.05 → Rs 18.95/L, an increase of Rs 18.90/L) recovers approximately PKR 1.5 billion of this annually — the first meaningful fiscal normalisation of kerosene pricing in years.

Section 4 · Supply
Fully domestically supplied — five active refineries, zero imports on record
All of Pakistan's kerosene consumption (~65,964 T in FY2024-25) is produced by domestic refineries. Kerosene is not recorded in EYB 2024-25 Table 2.12 (Import of Petroleum Products). It is an involuntary by-product of crude oil distillation — refineries cannot meaningfully suppress kerosene cuts without reducing overall throughput. ENAR's residue-upgrading facility has grown output approximately 3× over FY2019-24, making it now the likely largest single producer. Attock Refinery produces zero kerosene. EYB notes explicitly that ENAR's output "surpasses the volume of crude oil processed due to residue-upgradation facility" — a unique characteristic.

Structural surplus risk: As the market continues its ACGR decline of −5.8%, refineries will accumulate surplus kerosene cuts — a by-product they cannot easily suppress. This creates mild downward pressure on ex-refinery prices, which paradoxically provides a self-stabilising floor for consumer demand in off-grid areas even without explicit government subsidy.

Section 5 · Market Context & Outlook
Smallest product in the basket — politically sticky as a social fuel, fiscally unsustainable at scale
At 0.41% of Pakistan's total petroleum consumption, kerosene is the smallest energy product in the POL basket — yet it carries outsized political salience as the primary liquid fuel for off-grid rural households who cannot afford LPG or access the grid. Petrol (47%) and HSD (42.3%) together account for 89.3% of the basket. Kerosene is a rounding error by volume but a politically sensitive instrument by policy, with the government historically treating it as an untouchable social fuel subject to minimal taxation.

Structural Forces Shaping the Demand Outlook
NegativeRural electrification expanding grid access to previously off-grid kerosene users; BTM solar (26.8 GW installed base as of 2025) reducing household electricity cost further and accelerating grid-equivalent access.
NegativeGovernment LPG cylinder distribution subsidies directly substituting kerosene in cooking and heating — a policy deliberately targeting the same household segment.
NegativeDemographic shift: younger rural households more likely to adopt modern fuels; kerosene use concentrated in older, remoter, more conservative communities.
RiskMar–Apr 2025 Customs Duty escalation (Rs 0.05 → Rs 18.95/L) partially offsets ex-refinery price relief; further PDL or GST hikes could choke the 2024-25 price-led demand recovery and return market to accelerated contraction.
SupportBrent crude softness expected to persist through CY2025–26, keeping ex-refinery prices in the Rs 135–155/L range and consumer prices near Rs 165–175/L even with higher duties.
SupportLPG price spikes in winter months push marginal rural households back to kerosene — a recurring seasonal support pattern that creates a floor for demand.
SupportRefinery surplus dynamics: as kerosene market volume declines, ex-refinery prices face downward pressure from by-product oversupply — a self-stabilising mechanism that keeps consumer prices soft.

Policy prescription: Even in the bull scenario, kerosene consumption declines further — making this market a managed exit, not a growth story. The appropriate posture is an orderly fiscal normalisation: gradually raising PDL and applying a reduced GST rate over a 3–5 year schedule, while transitioning social protection for low-income households to targeted LPG or electricity vouchers. Blanket kerosene price concessions are an increasingly blunt and leaky instrument as the user base shrinks and the middle class — for whom kerosene has long been substituted — represents an ever-smaller share of remaining demand.
