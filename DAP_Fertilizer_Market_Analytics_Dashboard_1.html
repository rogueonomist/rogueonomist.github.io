Policy reading
Pakistani farmers pay well above the world price for DAP, they barely cut back even when it is expensive — except during the October–November planting rush, when price hikes bite noticeably harder. Policy design should treat peak-season pricing and off-season pricing as functionally different problems, not a single national price point.
Section B

Price relationship and the local premium

Since Pakistan imports most of its DAP, local prices track world prices over the long run — but always sit above them. This section isolates how much of that premium is tax, timing lag, and structural cost.

In plain English
Local price is always higher than the world price, by 48% on average. Some of that gap is completely normal (shipping, moving the product around the country, the margin shops need to make). A small slice is government tax (5%, only since 2023). Part of it isn't a real cost at all — it's a timing lag: when world prices jump suddenly, local prices take a few months to catch up, which makes the gap look bigger or smaller than it really is for a while.

Reading the correlation gap
Level correlation (0.81–0.93) is driven mostly by the shared long-run trend (global fertilizer cycle + rupee depreciation). Month-to-month return correlation is only ~0.30 — short-run pass-through is much looser, consistent with periodic subsidy intervention and inventory buffering. International price changes lead local retail changes by roughly four months.

Section C

Demand and the seasonal pattern

Fertilizer buying is driven almost entirely by the farming calendar. This section shows the annual picture, then isolates the typical-year seasonal cycle.

In plain English
Farmers buy roughly four times as much DAP in October–November (wheat planting season) as in January–February — that's just when the crop needs it. Even though price has risen roughly five-fold since 2015, the total amount farmers buy each year has stayed in roughly the same range. Fertilizer is a "must-buy" tied to the growing season, not a discretionary purchase.

Section D

Price elasticity of demand

How much does quantity demanded fall when price rises? Comparing seasons of the same calendar month across years, at different price levels, real (CPI-deflated) price, seasonally controlled.

In plain English
"Elasticity" asks: if price is 10% higher, how much less do people buy? A number close to zero means people barely change behaviour. Here the numbers are consistently low — roughly 6–8% less bought for every 10% higher price — confirming farmers don't have a real substitute for DAP. The surprising finding: during the October–November planting rush, that price-sensitivity roughly doubles.

Key correction from earlier analysis
October 2022 sits at 23% of normal offtake with stock cover at 441% of normal — supply was ample and piling up, not scarce. That is clean evidence of a genuine price-driven demand pullback at the highest price level in the series, not rationing. By contrast, Oct/Nov-2023 and Nov-2024 show low offtake and low stock cover together — genuine supply shortages tied to the 2023–24 import/LC restriction period. Excluding only those four genuinely supply-constrained months — rather than a coarser cut that drops all of 2022 — strengthens the peak elasticity estimate to −1.20 (p=0.014), up from an earlier, more conservative −1.11.
Section E

Subsidy cost-effectiveness

Modeled against the two live proposals: Punjab's Rs 3,000/bag Rabi-season discount, and the federal "Kissan Package" negotiated price of Rs 11,250/bag vs. a ~Rs 14,900 market price.

In plain English
If the government makes DAP cheaper, a lot of that money goes to farmers who would have bought it anyway — that's how subsidies work when people don't change their buying much. For every extra ton bought specifically because of the subsidy, it costs the government Rs 320,000 to Rs 590,000. Punjab's proposed Rs 20 billion budget looks large enough to cover the scheme even in the costlier scenarios. Whether the subsidy reaches the right farmers is an open question this data can't yet answer.

Targeting caveat
No farm-size or province-segmented offtake data exists in this project, so the ≤12-acre eligibility cap cannot be directly elasticity-tested. Regional retail price dispersion is low in normal years (~2–3% CV) — ruling out stable regional price gaps as the explanation for differential targeting — but it widened to 6.8–8.1% in 2022–24, consistent with hoarding/black-marketing during the supply-crisis years. Confirming whether the acreage cap is well-calibrated needs Agriculture Census farm-size data cross-referenced with district-level offtake.
Section F

Methodology and sources

Every figure on this dashboard traces to one of the sources below; caveats are stated where estimation, interpolation, or a proxy was required.

Price & demand data
Karandaaz weekly retail DAP price (national avg.), 2013–2026; NFDC/company monthly offtake, production, import, export and inventory balance sheet.
International benchmark
World Bank Pink Sheet DAP price (USD/mt); SBP monthly PKR/USD exchange rate.
CPI deflator
World Bank/IMF annual Pakistan inflation, interpolated to monthly — an approximation, not PBS's official monthly index.
Tax & subsidy facts
FBR circulars (0% customs duty throughout; 0% GST pre-Jul-2023, 5% after). Punjab and federal subsidy terms from current press reporting.
Limitations
What this dashboard cannot show
Stated plainly so downstream use doesn't overreach the data.
No freight invoices, customs assessment values, or dealer-margin surveys exist in-house — the "structural" premium component is a bundled residual, not a line-item cost build-up. No farm-size or province-segmented offtake data exists — smallholder-specific elasticity is not directly testable. The CPI deflator is an annual-to-monthly interpolation, not PBS's actual monthly index. Peak-season elasticity is estimated on a thin sample (23 observations, two months × ~11 years) — directionally robust but not precisely pinned down.
