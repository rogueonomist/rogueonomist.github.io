The main point

People now get electricity from more than just the grid. Rooftop solar cuts daytime grid use, batteries can move some solar power into the night, and air-conditioning pushes demand up after sunset. So Pakistan should plan for how much electricity the grid is still needed to supply each hour — instead of simply building as much generation as possible.

01 · The Big Finding

Pakistan's main power problem is not simply 'we need more power plants'

The harder problem is WHEN electricity is needed and WHO is still buying it from the grid. Rooftop solar cuts daytime grid sales. Batteries can move some of that power into the night. Air-conditioning pushes the biggest demand into a few evening and night hours. But old power-plant bills still have to be paid, even if the grid sells fewer units.

The biggest risk

People may use more electricity overall but buy less of it from the grid. If the grid has large fixed bills, fewer grid sales mean each remaining unit must carry more of those bills. Higher prices then push even more people toward solar, batteries and other options. Pakistan can therefore end up with too many contracted power plants and high electricity prices at the same time.

02 · How Much Electricity People Will Need

First estimate how much electricity people need. Then ask where it will come from.

Do not start by guessing grid sales. First estimate total electricity use. Then subtract power supplied by rooftop solar and batteries. What is left is what people need from the grid.

The simple calculation (Section 13) 1. Total electricity need = cooling + industry + all other uses
2. Electricity bought from the grid = total need minus electricity supplied by solar and batteries
3. The main power system must supply grid purchases plus the electricity lost while moving power through the network

Four Big Changes (Section 11)

  • Grid sales are no longer the same as total electricity use. People can use the same amount of electricity but buy fewer units from the grid because solar, batteries and private generation supply part of it.
  • The highest hourly demand and total yearly use no longer move together. A small change in the assumed daily pattern can change how many power plants seem necessary, even when yearly electricity use stays the same.
  • Cooling grows as more homes and businesses buy cooling equipment. It depends on how many air-conditioners and fans exist, how hot it is, and when people use them.
  • Solar helps least when the system often needs power most. Grid use can be lowest in sunny daytime hours and highest late at night, after solar stops producing.
Compare like with like

Two official cases use different annual electricity totals and different assumptions about how evenly power is used. So their 6.899 GW peak difference is not a clean comparison. If annual electricity use is held at the same 180.605 TWh, changing only the assumed usage pattern moves the peak from 35.547 GW to 28.635 GW. That is a 6.912 GW difference created by the assumption, not by people using more electricity.

As incomes rise, cooling use can rise quickly

When incomes rise, more people buy air-conditioners and refrigerators. That can make electricity demand rise quickly on hot evenings. The cheapest response is not always a new power plant. Pakistan should count how many cooling appliances are being added, estimate when they are used, and make efficient ACs and fans the standard. That can reduce bills and avoid building expensive capacity that is needed for only a few hours.

Why official registrations miss some solar

The 7.319 GW of registered net-metering is only a minimum known amount for registered systems. It does not include every solar system, because many systems do not export power, are not registered, or are fully off-grid. The official 8.12 GW projection for 2035 is already close to today's registered number, so it likely misses a large amount of solar that is already physically installed.

One battery cannot do two jobs at the same time (Section 23)

If a battery is being saved for backup, it cannot also be fully used to lower the grid peak at that same time. The same battery power must not be counted twice. Batteries used by homes and businesses may naturally reduce grid demand. Batteries promised to the grid through a coordinated program must also prove they can respond when called. The model should count each effect only once. Pakistan needs simple rules that let many small batteries work together for the grid, so battery growth is planned for rather than ignored.

06 · What the Grid Will Actually Need to Supply

Start with total electricity need, subtract solar and batteries, and the remainder is grid demand

Once solar and batteries are included, the amount people need from the grid can look very different from a simple forecast of past electricity sales.

The dangerous price cycle

Large fixed power bills push grid prices up → high prices make solar and batteries more attractive → people buy fewer grid units → the same fixed bills are divided over fewer units → grid prices rise again. Pakistan can therefore end up with too many contracted power plants and high electricity prices at the same time.

A battery's size is not enough — how long it lasts matters

A battery sold as a four-hour battery can provide full power for only 3.24 hours after normal limits and energy losses are included. If part of the battery is kept for backup, the usable time becomes even shorter. A battery emptied from 6pm to 10pm may have nothing left for the 11pm–midnight peak. Planning should therefore test batteries that last 2, 4, 6 and 8 hours, including difficult days and different locations on the grid.

When grid sales fall, the price per unit can rise

Who Should Pay the Extra Cost of Diamer Bhasha?

Under the official planning method, if a strategic project costs more than the cheapest system option, that extra cost is meant to be paid by the sponsoring government. The USD 3.0015 billion extra cost above the least-cost option should therefore not automatically be added to electricity consumers' power-plant bills — the cost still exists, but the government rather than electricity consumers would pay that strategic-policy premium.

The bottom line

Pakistan's main challenge is not simply the number of megawatts. It is when power is needed, how many units are still bought from the grid, and how much fixed cost must be paid.

Cooling is pushing demand higher at night. Solar is cutting daytime grid sales. Old fixed power-plant bills remain. Batteries are growing quickly outside the central plan. And new generation is useful only if the transmission network can deliver it. Pakistan therefore needs hour-by-hour planning that connects all five pieces, instead of planning mainly around the total size of power plants.
