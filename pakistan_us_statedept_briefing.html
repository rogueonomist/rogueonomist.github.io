The Structural Argument

Why Pakistan is at an Inflection Point

Pakistan is a lower-middle-income country that has been structurally trapped at that threshold for thirty years. The trap is self-reinforcing: low investment → low productivity → low exports → fiscal stress → sovereign borrowing crowds out private credit → lower investment. The country has averaged 4.7% annual GDP growth since 1952, but has never sustained the 6–7% growth required to transition income classes.

Three things are different in 2025–2026. First, the macroeconomic stabilization is real — inflation has collapsed from 38% to 3%, the policy rate has dropped from 22% to 11%, and IMF program discipline has restored a degree of fiscal credibility. Second, the geopolitical context has shifted: India–Pakistan tensions, the India–US trade crisis, and the Trump administration's strategic recalibration have all elevated Pakistan's commercial significance to Washington. Third, the resource opportunity is now financeable: Reko Diq's $74B in free cash flow over 37 years, Pakistan's critical minerals endowment, and a July 2025 US–Pakistan trade framework all point toward a qualitatively different bilateral commercial relationship.

The question for US commercial diplomats is not whether Pakistan is worth engaging. The question is how — which sectors, which instruments, which institutional mechanisms, and which conditions must be in place for US businesses to operate at scale.

Middle Income Transition

What Pakistan Needs to Do to Cross the Threshold

The World Bank's upper-middle-income threshold is approximately $4,465 GNI per capita. Pakistan's current per capita income is $1,901 — meaning the country needs to roughly triple per-capita income to graduate. At 7.5% annual GDP growth with 2% population growth (net per-capita growth of ~5.5%), Pakistan could achieve upper-middle-income status by 2048–2050.

The challenge is that Pakistan has never sustained 7%+ growth for more than 3–4 consecutive years. The structural requirements are clear: raise investment/GDP from 14.5% to 25%+, expand merchandise exports from $40.8B to $130B+ by 2035, and build a manufacturing base capable of absorbing 5–7 million new workers annually.

The Crowding-Out Mechanism

Government borrowing from the commercial banking sector consumed 72% of total bank lending in FY2023. With the sovereign offering T-bill yields that dwarf risk-adjusted returns on industrial lending, banks have rationally exited private credit. Private credit as a share of GDP is significantly below the regional average — and declining. Pakistan's banking system is financing the government's consumption deficit with capital that should be building factories.

Section II — Power & Energy

The Circular Debt Machine

How Private Power Became Public Debt

Pakistan's power sector crisis is routinely described as a supply problem that became a financing problem. This framing is wrong. The correct sequence: a contract design problem — dollar-denominated, return-of-equity-guaranteed IPP agreements signed in the 1990s and 2000s — created sovereign risk that was misclassified as commercial risk, accumulated into a liability stock, and now constrains every macro policy option available.

Capacity charges — the guaranteed payments to Independent Power Producers (IPPs) regardless of dispatch — rose from Rs. 793B in FY2021 to Rs. 1.9T in FY2024, a 140% increase against nominal GDP growth of ~57%. Coal and nuclear capacity charges alone grew from 37% to 59% of total capacity charges, creating an FCY-exposed liability structure that Pakistan neither controls nor can hedge.

The Arithmetic

Pakistan's average industrial electricity tariff of Rs. 50–60/kWh makes export-oriented manufacturing structurally uncompetitive. A textile mill in Faisalabad pays 2.3x more per kWh than its counterpart in Dhaka. A steel mill pays 2.7x more than in Vietnam. No amount of labor cost advantage, tariff preference, or proximity to port compensates for this differential at scale.

Generation Mix (Installed Capacity)

Hydro (27%) and RLNG/Gas (25%) dominate. Nuclear (5%) and solar (6%) are growing. The 2025–2030 plan targets 60% renewables in new additions — but without circular debt resolution, no new investment will close.

Reform Prescription — What Must Change
  • IPP Contract Renegotiation: Convert remaining dollar-linked capacity payments to PKR, reduce guaranteed ROEs from 15–17% to 12–14%, align with actual dispatch economics.
  • Circular Debt Resolution Vehicle: Establish a Power Sector Debt Resolution Authority (PSDRA) to consolidate PHPL liabilities, issue 15-year amortization bonds with a dedicated levy.
  • Competitive Procurement: Replace negotiated capacity contracts with transparent auction-based procurement — NEPRA already has the framework; political will is the constraint.
  • Energy Cross-Subsidy Rationalization: Phase out general subsidies; target direct income support to vulnerable consumers, not tariff suppression that distorts the entire sector.
  • Renewable Fast-Track: Solar and wind at $0.025–0.035/kWh landed cost are the fastest path to affordable industrial electricity. Remove grid access barriers and wheeling restrictions.

Strategic Frame for US Commercial Diplomats

Pakistan's energy sector is simultaneously its greatest structural liability and greatest commercial opportunity for US businesses. The July 2025 trade deal explicitly unlocks US participation in oil and gas development — but the bigger prize is the power sector reform dividend. Every percentage point reduction in industrial electricity tariffs translates directly into manufacturing competitiveness: at $0.10/kWh, Pakistan's textile and light manufacturing sectors become globally competitive. US investors in renewable energy, smart grid, and LNG supply chains are not just commercial participants — they are structural reform agents enabling Pakistan's industrial transition.

Geopolitical Context

The July 2025 deal did not emerge in a commercial vacuum. Commercial diplomacy has become strategic diplomacy by another name — and US businesses are the beneficiaries of a geopolitical realignment that has dramatically elevated Pakistan's commercial importance to Washington.

Commercial Diplomacy Context

The Trump Administration's Pakistan Calculus

Trade Surplus Correction Imperative

Pakistan's $3B trade surplus with the US in 2024 triggered a 29% reciprocal tariff under Trump's April 2025 measures. Unlike with India (50% tariff), the Pakistan negotiation concluded favorably — a function of Pakistan's strategic cooperation on regional security, willingness to offer energy sector access, and reduced strategic confrontation posture.

India–Pakistan Realignment Dividend

The May 2025 India-Pakistan ceasefire, US mediation role, and subsequent India-US trade rupture (50% tariffs on Indian goods) have created a window. Pakistan has positioned itself as a cooperative, pliable partner for US strategic interests — translating diplomatic capital into commercial concessions in the July 2025 deal.

Critical Mineral Supply Chain Security

Trump administration's critical minerals strategy treats Pakistan as a priority supply chain diversification partner — an alternative to China-dominated supply chains for copper, lithium, rare earths, and antimony. The $500M US Strategic Metals–FWO deal (September 2025) and Reko Diq financing interest are both products of this strategic frame.

Business Environment Analysis

Key Friction Points for US Companies

⚖️
Tax on Turnover, Not Profit
Pakistan's minimum tax regime imposes 1.25–1.5% tax on gross revenue regardless of profitability. US companies operating at thin margins or in startup mode are disproportionately impacted. ABC's core demand: shift to profit-based taxation.
🔄
Profit Repatriation Delays
State Bank of Pakistan historically imposed caps and delays on dividend repatriation. While improved under IMF program conditions, uncertainty persists. US companies require formal statutory guarantees on repatriation timelines.
🏗️
Illicit Trade & Counterfeit Competition
Estimated 30–40% of Pakistan's consumer goods market is supplied by smuggled or counterfeit goods — particularly electronics, tobacco, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals. US brands lose significant market share to unregulated, tariff-evading competitors.
💻
Digital Presence Proceeds Tax (2025)
Pakistan's 2025 budget introduced a tax on digital services revenues from companies without a physical presence — directly targeting US tech firms (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon). The US-Pakistan Business Council formally objected. Status: under active renegotiation.
📋
Super Tax & Policy Instability
Pakistan's 10% super tax on large companies (imposed 2022) remains in effect with only marginal reduction promised in FY25–26 budget. Combined with minimum tax, effective rates on US companies often exceed 40–45%. ABC proposes broader, faster reduction.
🛃
Regulatory Fragmentation & Provincial Complexity
Post-18th Amendment devolution created provincial regulatory regimes across labor, environment, and land. US companies operating nationally face 4 provincial + federal compliance requirements — each with different standards and enforcement approaches.
ABC Budget Proposals FY2025–26

What US Business is Demanding from Government

ABC's Priority Reform Demands (Submitted Pre-Budget 2025-26)
  1. Profit-Based Taxation: Replace minimum tax on turnover with profit-based corporate tax. US companies with high capex and thin margins are disproportionately penalized under turnover-based regimes — explicitly discouraging capital-intensive investment.
  2. Super Tax Phase-Out: Reduce and eventually eliminate the 10% super tax on large corporates; apply relief across the corporate sector (not just below a revenue threshold as currently structured). ABCs concern: limited relief does not restore investor confidence at scale.
  3. Counterfeit/Illicit Trade Enforcement: Strengthen customs enforcement, establish a dedicated Anti-Illicit Trade Authority with US technical assistance, implement track-and-trace for pharmaceuticals and electronics. Level the playing field for legitimate US brands.
  4. Long-Term Tax Certainty: Hard-code 5-year tax policy stability windows for new investments above $100M. "Every serious investor asks: what will my tax exposure be, and will the rules change after I invest?" (P@SHA Chairman, 2025). Policy-coded continuity is the precondition for capital.
  5. High Tariff Rationalization: High import tariffs on finished goods (cosmetics, electronics, medical devices) have created incentives for smuggling rather than legal import. US firms with branded products lose to unregulated channels. Reducing tariffs to revenue-neutral levels cuts smuggling while improving compliance.
  6. Digital Services Tax Reconsideration: Digital Presence Proceeds Tax treats foreign digital services companies as if they have physical nexus — without providing commensurate protections. Aligned with OECD Pillar One framework would be preferable and would not drive US tech firms away from Pakistan's market.
Government Response (June 2025)

Finance Minister Aurangzeb's response to the ABC delegation: acknowledged the scale and depth of US investment as "significant and valuable," committed to a structured Tax Policy Office with an Advisory Panel for ongoing consultation. The Finance Minister explicitly welcomed ABC's input and acknowledged accommodation of some proposals in the FY25–26 budget. The institutional channel is working — but execution pace remains the constraint.

Risk Assessment

Security Environment
Balochistan insurgency has directly impacted Reko Diq (Barrick slowed development in April 2026 citing security escalation). Khyber Pakhtunkhwa faces TTP-linked activity. Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad — where 90% of US businesses operate — are categorically safer. Risk is geographic, not systemic.

Pakistan has had 5 regime changes in 10 years, each bringing policy reversals. Market Size & Demographics
257 million population with median age of 22. Urban middle class of 40–50 million growing at 3–4% annually. Smartphone penetration at 65%+ creates a digital-native consumer base. Pakistan is the 5th-largest country in the world — the consumer market opportunity is fundamentally under-appreciated by US businesses.

Ongoing Constraint
Midstream Gap: No Processing Infrastructure
Pakistan's REE sector currently limited to raw mineral extraction with virtually no functioning midstream processing infrastructure. Building a poly-metallic refinery is Phase 2 of the USSM deal — but requires 3–5 years and $300–500M in capex.
Policy Recommendation for State Dept

Push for a Minerals Development Framework Agreement (MDFA) — a bilateral treaty that locks in investor protections, royalty rates, environmental standards, and dispute resolution mechanisms for the entire minerals sector. The current ad hoc deal-by-deal approach creates reputational and legal risks for US firms. A formal framework, modeled on US–DRC minerals agreement discussions, would give US investors the certainty to commit capital at scale to Pakistan's mineral sector.

US Financial Exposure — The EXIM Opportunity
🏦

EXIM Bank is reviewing financing proposals of $500M–$1B for Reko Diq Phase 1. This would be one of EXIM's largest-ever single-project commitments in South Asia — and directly supports Fluor Corporation (US EPCM contractor) engagement.
🌍
IFC ($300–650M) & DFI Stack
IFC providing $300M in debt financing; additional DFI stack from ADB, Export Development Canada, and JBIC. Total project financing: $3B target (close expected Q3 2025 — now delayed due to security review).
🏗️
Fluor Corporation (US EPCM)
Selected April 2025 as lead Engineering, Procurement and Construction Management contractor — a major US industrial company's largest active contract in Pakistan. Fluor's involvement creates a strong US commercial anchor for the entire project.
April 2026 — Security Crisis

Barrick Mining (renamed from Barrick Gold) announced in April 2026 that it is slowing development activity until mid-2027 citing "escalation of security risks and increased security incidents" in Pakistan and the region. The previously targeted first production date of end-2028 will be significantly delayed. Capital budget estimates are expected to increase substantially. This is the single most critical risk issue for US commercial diplomacy on critical minerals — resolving the Balochistan security environment is a prerequisite for Reko Diq's commercial viability.

Critical Path for Reko Diq Success
  • Security Framework: Pakistan Army must establish a dedicated security perimeter and protocol for the mine site — comparable to Saindak's operational security model. US diplomatic pressure for military commitment to project security is essential.
  • Project Finance Close: $3B limited recourse financing must close before major construction can recommence. EXIM Bank's $500M–$1B tranche is the keystone — without it, the DFI stack does not assemble.
  • Balochistan Compact: Provincial government's 25% stake must be accompanied by a genuine social compact — royalties to local communities, employment quotas for Baloch workers, and transparent revenue sharing to prevent political sabotage of project.
  • Saudi Manara Stake: Saudi Manara Minerals is seeking 10–20% from Pakistan government's 25% stake. Completing this transaction would provide Pakistan with $500M–$1B in upfront proceeds while bringing a GCC strategic partner into the project — reducing geopolitical risk.
  • Value Chain Development: Pakistan should negotiate offtake arrangements that include a commitment to midstream processing — copper concentrate smelting and refining — rather than exporting raw concentrate. This doubles Pakistan's revenue per tonne.
Macro Impact of Full Reko Diq Development

At full Phase 1 production (2029+), Reko Diq would generate ~$3–4B in annual export revenues — adding 7–10% to Pakistan's total merchandise export base. Phase 2 doubles this to $6–8B/year. The mine could become Pakistan's largest single source of foreign exchange earnings within a decade, fundamentally altering the country's structural balance of payments position.

Pakistan's 0.7M tonne cold storage capacity vs. India's 30M tonnes and Vietnam's 5M tonnes. A $500M investment in cold storage infrastructure would reduce post-harvest losses by $2–3B annually, directly increasing exportable surplus in agriculture — Pakistan's second-largest export category.
🚢
Port Modernization
Port Qasim expansion and Karachi container terminal modernization require $1–2B in investment. US port operators (Dubai-based DP World has existing presence) and equipment providers have strong opportunities. A US-backed counter to CPEC port infrastructure would carry strategic weight.
🚛

Pakistan's e-commerce boom ($6B+, growing 35%/yr) is constrained by last-mile logistics. Amazon Logistics (in India), FedEx, and DHL can all significantly expand Pakistan operations. China+1 Thesis

The global "China+1" diversification strategy — driven by US tariffs, supply chain risk mitigation, and ESG considerations — has primarily benefited Vietnam, Mexico, India, and Bangladesh. Pakistan is structurally eligible but operationally excluded due to energy costs, security perceptions, and regulatory friction. If energy tariffs normalize to $0.08–0.10/kWh and policy stability is codified, Pakistan's labor cost advantage (40–50% below Vietnam, 60% below India for semi-skilled manufacturing) would make it one of the most compelling China+1 destinations globally.

US Strategic Interests in Pakistan's Connectivity
🛡️
Counter-CPEC Infrastructure Positioning
The Blue Dot Network (US-Japan-Australia infrastructure certification) and Lobito Corridor model provide templates for US-backed connectivity corridors in Pakistan. A US-certified, commercially sustainable transport and logistics corridor through Pakistan diversifies away from CPEC dependency without confrontational displacement.
🌾
Afghanistan Agricultural Trade Corridor
Pakistan is the primary transit route for Afghan agricultural products (dried fruits, nuts, saffron). A US-supported agri-logistics corridor through Pakistan's border regions creates economic incentives for border stability while enabling US agricultural technology transfer.
⚡
SAARC Energy Grid Revival
The stalled SAARC (South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation) electricity interconnection would allow Pakistan to export hydropower surplus to India and import during deficit periods. US grid technology, smart grid systems, and DFC financing for cross-border transmission lines represent a major commercial opportunity.
🌐
Digital Silk Road Alternative
China's Digital Silk Road has laid fiber optic cables, 5G infrastructure, and cloud data centers across Pakistan. US digital infrastructure players (Google Subsea cables, Amazon Cloud, Microsoft Azure) can establish countervailing presence — particularly in data center, cloud computing, and 5G spectrum infrastructure. The STZA provides the regulatory framework.
The Overarching Opportunity

Pakistan is the only country in the world that simultaneously borders China, India, Iran, Afghanistan, and has maritime access to the Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean. Its geography is a strategic multiplier. A US-Pakistan commercial partnership that is anchored in energy, minerals, digital infrastructure, and logistics does not just generate bilateral trade flows — it restructures the economic geography of South Asia in ways that align with American strategic priorities for the next 25 years.
