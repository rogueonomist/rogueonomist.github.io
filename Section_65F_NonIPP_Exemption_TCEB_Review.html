Section 65F Non-IPP Extension: Does the Business Case Hold Up?
A review of SECMC's July 2026 proposal to extend the Section 65F income-tax exemption from IPP coal sales to non-IPP (industrial/merchant) sales — tested against SECMC's own scale-up roadmap and TCEB's own electrification mandate, rather than against a single transitional year.
Subject document: "Unlocking Pakistan's Energy Potential: 65F Extension to Non-IPP" (SECMC, July 2026) Prepared: July 2026 Basis: SECMC MYT Determination, TCEB Roadmap analysis, SECMC's own capacity & electrification plans
Executive Verdict
The case as submitted is internally inconsistent — and once corrected, mostly self-resolving
SECMC argues, in the same deck, that scale will make Thar coal ever-cheaper and that it is uncompetitive today without a tax exemption. Both can be true only if "today" is a single, cherry-picked transitional year. A realistic multi-year view — using SECMC's own committed capacity roadmap and TCEB's own mandated electrification programme — shows the competitiveness gap closes on its own within roughly four years, with or without the exemption.

Bottom line
A permanent, blanket 65F extension to all non-IPP sales is not supported by the underlying economics. The narrow case that does hold up is for a short, milestone-linked bridge covering roughly FY2026–FY2029 — the exact window in which SECMC's own scale-up and TCEB's own mandated electrification programme have not yet fully landed. Beyond that window, the deck's own numbers, honestly extended, argue against relief rather than for it.

Critical Review
Where the business case breaks down
Four distinct problems — each sufficient on its own to warrant rejecting the ask as framed.
The deck argues both sides of the same coin
SECMC's own scale-economics narrative — repeated across its tariff filings — is that capacity payment per tonne has already fallen 46% from Phase I COD to the Phase III contract-stage review (USD 31.90/t → USD 17.02/t), purely from spreading fixed costs over a larger base, before any electrification benefit is even counted. The 65F deck simultaneously argues Thar coal will keep getting cheaper and that it cannot survive today without a permanent tax shield. Both claims can be true only if "today" means the single highest-cost year of a 30-year tariff — which is exactly the year the deck uses.
"With exemption, every industry is competitive" is not an economic argument — it's a tautology
Removing a cost from a price comparison always makes the price look better; that is true of literally any tax, on any industry, at any margin. It is not evidence of a market failure, an infant-industry case, or a national-interest imperative — it is arithmetic. The deck needs to show why Thar coal specifically deserves this treatment when cement, textiles, and every other trade-exposed sector could make an identical argument. As framed, it proves too much to prove anything.
The IPP exemption already did its job — extending it to merchant sales is a different policy problem
TCEB's own determination orders record that IPP-sale tariffs carry no provision for income tax at all — "if the Petitioner is obligated to pay any tax, the exact amount will be reimbursed by the off-taker" (Phase-I COD Determination). The 2011 FBR SEZ notification granting the exemption was framed as parity with IPP-style incentives precisely because IPP sales sit inside a regulated, cost-plus, single-buyer, sovereign-guaranteed structure where a tax charge simply inflates the tariff paid by consumers. Non-IPP sales are ordinary merchant transactions — SECMC negotiates price with cement plants, captive power plants, and fertilizer companies the way any commercial supplier does. There is no consumer tariff to protect from a tax mark-up. Treating the two as the same policy problem is a category error, not an extension of an existing principle.
The comparison is apples-to-oranges: a single bad year vs. a levelized, efficiency-adjusted future
The 3.98 USD/MMBTU mine-mouth figure is Phase III's front-loaded first year — debt service and capacity payment are heaviest before the 11.2→20 MTPA scale-up dilutes them, and before TCEB's own MYT-mandated electrification programme (grid dewatering, EV haul pilots, IPCC/conveyor feasibility) delivers any savings at all. A non-IPP industrial buyer signs a multi-year offtake, not a one-year contract. The right comparison is the multi-year trajectory below — not the peak of the curve.

Jurisdictional Note
This is a federal tax question, not a TCEB tariff question
Worth stating plainly, especially given TCEB's own recent position on regulatory independence.
Where 65F actually sits
Section 65F is a provision of the Income Tax Ordinance, 2001 — a federal statute administered by the Federal Board of Revenue under the Finance Division. TCEB's mandate under the Thar Coal & Energy Board Act, 2011 (Section 5(m)) is coal tariff determination — a distinct and provincial competence. Extending 65F to non-IPP sales is a federal fiscal-policy decision. TCEB can and should supply the analytical evidence (as this review attempts to do), but the ask itself belongs with FBR and the Finance Division, not with the tariff-setting process.
A point of internal consistency
TCEB has, in recent correspondence, firmly asserted its own independence from federal Power Division involvement in coal-pricing matters, on the grounds that this is a provincial subject under the TCEB Act. There is a real tension in TCEB actively championing a federal tax carve-out on SECMC's behalf while simultaneously resisting federal involvement in its own tariff domain. The cleaner institutional posture is: TCEB provides the cost evidence; FBR/Finance Division decides the tax policy question on its own merits.
Recommendation
What actually makes sense
Four options, in order of preference — none of them the blanket, permanent, sector-wide exemption as submitted.
Not Recommended
Permanent blanket 65F extension
  • Locks in relief long after the underlying cost gap closes (2030 at the latest, per every scenario tested).
  • Removes the pressure to actually deliver the electrification programme TCEB has already mandated.
  • Sets a sector-wide precedent: any trade-exposed industry can make the identical argument.
Preferred Option
Time-bound, milestone-linked bridge
  • Relief through FY2026–FY2029 only, matching the window where the gap is real under every tested scenario.
  • Conditioned on delivery milestones already in the MYT Determination: HESCO grid connection, EV pilot results, IPCC/conveyor BFS.
  • Mandatory sunset and joint TCEB/FBR review at the next MYT cycle — no automatic renewal.
Alternative
Formula-based, self-terminating relief
  • Relief scales with the actual observed gap between Thar's landed cost and a published import index (e.g., McCloskey RB1).
  • Automatically shrinks to zero as the gap closes — no discretionary renewal decision needed.
  • Avoids a flat-rate subsidy that overpays once competitiveness is achieved.
Complementary, Regardless of Tax Decision
Attack the cost, not the tax
  • Fast-track tariff recognition for verified electrification savings (grid, EV, IPCC) — the lever that closes the gap for good.
  • Treat the Fauji Fertilizer coal-to-urea case as a targeted, project-specific decision on its own food-security merits — not bundled into a blanket exemption for cement, CPP, and gasification buyers alike.
