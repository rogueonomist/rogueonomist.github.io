Executive Summary

The electricity system has a heat problem that tariff policy pretends does not exist.

Pakistan's two largest urban electricity systems — LESCO serving Lahore and K-Electric serving Karachi — are running at their structural limits during summer. Twenty-six months of ISMO operational data, combined with NEPRA's fuel cost adjustment decisions, allow us to quantify what the grid absorbs during extreme heat and express it in terms that matter for policy: megawatts, consumers, and degrees Celsius.

The headline number is 1,559 MW — the difference between LESCO's average demand during the June 2024 heatwave month and its non-summer baseline. That is the energy equivalent of 1.5 Tarbela units running continuously, consumed almost entirely by residential and commercial air conditioning. Expressed per consumer, it translates to 216 additional kWh in a single peak month — roughly doubling the electricity bill for a typical Lahore household compared to February.

Karachi's picture is more complex and was, until this analysis, understated. ISMO data captures only what K-Electric purchases from the national grid (CPPA imports), ignoring KE's own gas and coal generation — roughly 1,447 MW in peak summer 2024, from NEPRA's own fuel cost adjustment data. The corrected KE total system demand peaks at approximately 2,651 MW in June 2024, with a heat-driven uplift of 557 MW over baseline. This generates an additional 125 kWh per domestic consumer per peak month.

The marginal temperature coefficient — how much additional electricity each degree of heat demands — converges to the same value for both cities: 13.9 kWh per domestic consumer per month per °C. This is not a coincidence. It is the signature of a common AC physics that transcends city, climate zone, and income group. What differs between Lahore and Karachi is not the slope of the heat response but the intercept: Karachi's year-round warmth means consumers draw 4–5 kWh more per day at any given temperature, because air conditioning is a baseline load, not a seasonal surge.

"Pakistan's summers are becoming grid emergencies — not slowly, but in 48-hour heat spikes that expose every planning gap simultaneously: insufficient distribution headroom, absent demand-response signals, and a tariff structure that treats July and January as equivalent."

The policy response — Time-of-Use tariffs, DSM programs quantified in megawatts rather than aspiration, and a cooling infrastructure investment framework — is technically straightforward. What has been missing is the analytical foundation to force the conversation. This brief provides it.

Section 1

The heat problem is worsening and the grid is absorbing costs it was not designed to carry.

In June 2024, temperatures in Karachi reached 47.2°C on June 25 — driving a heatwave that killed 568 people and hospitalised 7,905 across Sindh. In June 2025, Lahore recorded 46°C on June 9, its hottest day in 18 years. These are not statistical anomalies; they are a trend. Pakistan was its ninth warmest year on record in 2024, and July 2024 was the second-hottest July in 64 years of recorded data.

The electricity grid absorbs the thermal stress that buildings cannot. Where houses lack insulation, where urban heat islands concentrate in dense low-income settlements, where refrigerative cooling is the only mechanism available — every degree of additional heat becomes a grid problem. The demand surge is not gradual; it arrives within hours as temperatures cross the 40°C threshold and AC units switch from cycling to continuous operation.

Pakistan's installed generation capacity of 46.6 GW sounds adequate against a peak demand of roughly 30,000 MW. But capacity numbers hide the distribution problem: not enough power reaches Lahore feeders at the right voltage when LESCO is drawing 4,077 MW on a June midnight. The constraint is not national — it is urban and locational, and it has been getting worse every year that the heat season extends earlier in the calendar.

This brief is grounded in the only operational data that captures this reality at hourly resolution: 26 months of ISMO market statistics covering every hour from February 2024 through April 2026. The data is not perfect — May through September 2025 is missing from the CPPA-G public database, and K-Electric's own-generation is not captured in ISMO records. But the available data is sufficient to establish the quantitative case.

Two structural facts are immediately visible. First, LESCO's summer-to-winter swing (1,942 MW in February to 3,817 MW in June) is extreme by any standard — a 97% increase driven almost entirely by air conditioning. Second, KE's swing is more muted in absolute terms (1,782 MW to 2,651 MW, a 49% increase) but this understates the heat effect because Karachi consumers use AC year-round and the city's mild winters mask the marginal summer addition.

The non-summer months are not neutral either. October 2024 draws 2,850 MW for LESCO — still 47% above February — as the grid transitions out of peak cooling season. And the April 2026 readings (LESCO 2,578 MW, KE CPPA alone at 2,064 MW) show spring demand rising structurally across both territories, likely as heat events push earlier into the calendar year.

Section 2

Extreme heat produces a flat-to-inverted load curve. The grid is busiest at midnight, not at noon.

The most counter-intuitive finding in the ISMO data is the shape of the load profile during the June 2024 heatwave month. In a normal grid, peak demand tracks the working day — morning ramp, afternoon plateau, evening peak, overnight trough. What the June 2024 LESCO data shows is the opposite: demand peaks at midnight (H00: 4,077 MW) and barely dips to a trough at noon (H11: 3,559 MW). The difference between the highest and lowest hour is just 518 MW — a 13% swing across 24 hours.

This is the AC-all-night signature of sustained extreme heat. When outdoor temperatures remain above 38°C at 2 AM, air conditioning units never cycle off. The behavioural response — "set the thermostat and leave it" — turns a diurnal demand pattern into a near-constant block load. Load factor (the ratio of minimum to maximum hourly demand) climbs from 62% in February to 87% in June. The grid that was sized for peaks now must run at peak continuously.

Section 3

ISMO data captured one-third of Karachi's summer demand. The corrected picture changes the policy calculus entirely.

K-Electric is Pakistan's only vertically integrated utility — it generates its own power, operates its own grid, and purchases the balance from the national CPPA pool. ISMO's market statistics only record the CPPA purchase side. In summer 2024, that was roughly 1,004 MW per hour on average — a number that, in isolation, suggested Karachi was drawing trivially from the national grid compared to Lahore's 3,817 MW.

That reading is deeply misleading. NEPRA's fuel cost adjustment decision for July 2024 records 1,076 GWh of KE's own generation dispatched that month — an average of 1,447 MW per hour from KE's gas and coal plants (BQPS-I, BQPS-II, BQPS-III). Adding private supplier capacity of approximately 200 MW, KE's total system demand in peak summer 2024 was approximately 2,651 MW — not 1,004 MW. The CPPA-only reading understated Karachi's heat burden by a factor of 2.6.

The supply mix is also shifting structurally. By April 2026, KE's CPPA imports had risen to 2,064 MW on average — nearly double the summer 2024 average — as NTDC interconnection capacity expanded from 1,600 MW toward 2,050 MW and KE substituted costlier own-generation with cheaper national-grid supply. In FY 2023-24, KE's own generation was 42% of total system dispatch; by early 2026 the CPPA share had likely crossed 65%. This transition is commercially rational but introduces new transmission risk on NTDC's southwestern corridors.

Section 4

The heat burden per household: 216 kWh in Lahore, 125 kWh in Karachi — a divergence that reflects climate, not comfort.

Translating grid megawatts into household kWh is the step most policy documents skip. The arithmetic is straightforward but the implications are not. LESCO's 1,559 MW heat delta, run across 720 hours in a 30-day peak month and distributed over 5.2 million domestic consumers, produces an incremental 216 kWh per household in June versus the non-summer average. At current domestic tariff rates of approximately Rs. 35–50/kWh in the upper slabs, that is Rs. 7,560–10,800 of additional electricity cost in a single month — on top of an already elevated baseline bill.

KE's 125 kWh per-consumer heat increment is smaller in absolute terms, but the correct comparison must account for Karachi's structurally higher baseline. The average non-summer KE consumption per domestic consumer is approximately 471 kWh/month (against 313 kWh for LESCO), because Karachi is warm year-round and AC runs even in January. The heat uplift for Karachi (+27% above baseline) is smaller than Lahore's (+69%) not because Karachi is less affected by heat — the 47.2°C June 2024 peak disproves that — but because Lahore households start from a much lower winter base and then spike violently.

The implication for affordability is severe. The consumer who reduces consumption in winter to manage bills has nowhere to hide in summer: demand is not discretionary when temperatures are 44°C and dwelling in an unventilated house carries genuine health risk. Pakistan's lifeline and protected consumer categories provide some tariff shielding for sub-200-unit monthly users, but those thresholds are systematically breached in peak summer months even by lower-middle-income households.

Affordability pressure

Pakistan's lifeline consumer threshold is 200 kWh/month. A typical LESCO domestic consumer averaging 313 kWh in non-summer months is already above protected status. In June, the same household draws 529 kWh — consuming at rates that incur the upper residential slab charges. Heat is not a choice; the tariff structure does not recognise that distinction.

Section 5

The temperature coefficient: both cities respond at the same rate — 13.9 kWh per consumer per degree per month.

Running an OLS regression of monthly average system demand against climatological monthly mean temperatures for 2024 yields a result that is striking for its symmetry. LESCO produces a slope of 100 MW per degree Celsius (R² = 0.86, p = 0.0003 — strongly significant). Normalised to per-consumer terms over a 30-day month, the coefficient is 13.9 kWh per domestic consumer per degree.

KE's regression is weaker by conventional measures (R² = 0.41, p = 0.064) — not because the heat response is absent but because Karachi's monthly temperature range is only 10°C (20–31°C) versus Lahore's 21°C swing (15–36°C). When temperature barely varies across months, the regression has little leverage to isolate the signal. But normalising KE's slope to per-consumer terms produces the same number: 13.9 kWh per consumer per °C per month, or 0.46 kWh per consumer per day per °C.

This convergence is the most important single finding in this analysis. Two cities with entirely different climates, utility structures, and demand levels arrive at the same marginal AC response per household. The mechanism is universal: a degree of additional heat demands approximately 20–25 minutes of extra runtime from a 1-ton air conditioner. That translates directly to 0.46 kWh regardless of geography. What differs is the intercept — at any given temperature, Karachi consumers draw 4–5 kWh per day more than Lahore consumers, because cooling is a baseline function rather than a seasonal surge in the coastal city.

Interpretation note

The April 2024 LESCO outlier — actual demand 648 MW below the regression line — is analytically significant. It demonstrates that demand does not respond linearly to temperature in real time. At 28°C, many households have not yet switched AC units to continuous operation; the behavioural trigger appears to be somewhere between 30°C and 33°C. This implies that a 1°C increase near the 32–35°C range (the threshold zone) produces a larger marginal response than the regression slope suggests — demand is convex in temperature near the thermal stress threshold.

Section 6

Four interventions — priced, sequenced, and expressed in megawatts rather than aspiration.

Recommendation 01
Introduce summer Time-of-Use tariffs for peak night hours
Both LESCO and KE show demand peaking at midnight, not noon, during heat months. A summer peak window surcharge of Rs. 8–12/kWh for 10 PM to 2 AM (June–August) would create the first honest price signal for heat-driven consumption. The objective is not revenue — it is to shift pre-cooling, smart AC scheduling, and discretionary loads away from the hours when the grid is most constrained. India's ToU pilots in Maharashtra produced 8–12% peak hour load reduction within two years of rollout.
Target: 200–350 MW peak demand reduction in Year 1
Recommendation 02
Quantify the DSM program in MW, not in households
LESCO's 1,559 MW heat delta is operationally equivalent to 1.5 Tarbela units running around the clock for the entire summer. If NEPRA-approved DSM programs — efficient fan replacement, inverter AC subsidies, cool-roof incentives — could shave 20% of this heat delta, that is 312 MW of avoided peak capacity. At standard NEPRA capacity cost benchmarks of Rs. 80,000–120,000/kW, deferring 312 MW of new capacity investment represents Rs. 25–37 billion in avoided cost. The business case exists; the institutional mandate for DSM to quantify itself in these terms does not yet.
Target: 300–400 MW demand reduction from efficiency programs over 3 years
Recommendation 03
Mandate heat-season transmission planning for NTDC southwestern corridor
KE's CPPA imports nearly doubled between summer 2024 (~1,004 MW average) and April 2026 (~2,064 MW average) as NTDC interconnection expanded. May–September 2025 ISMO data is absent from the public database — we do not know what KE's peak summer 2025 import looked like at the height of the 46°C June 2025 heat wave in Lahore. NTDC must publish a confirmed peak import record for the Karachi corridor and conduct a heat-season stress test of the 500kV and 220kV links against worst-case KE demand. The current gap in public data is itself a governance failure.
Risk: NTDC corridor constraint during coincident heatwave in Lahore and Karachi
Recommendation 04
Revise the protected consumer threshold with a heat-season carve-out
The 200-unit protected consumer threshold is built for a normal climate that no longer exists in Pakistani summers. A household consuming 200 kWh in February can breach the threshold in June without any change in its economic circumstances — purely because of mandatory cooling loads. A heat-season adjustment that raises the protected consumer threshold to 350 kWh for June, July, and August, or that treats heat-increment consumption at protected rates, would cost the system relatively little in revenue terms while meaningfully protecting lower-income households from tariff shock. NEPRA should commission an affordability analysis using the 0.46 kWh/°C/consumer coefficient as the technical basis for threshold setting.

The forcing close
Pakistan's electricity infrastructure is not designed for the summers it now routinely experiences. The 13.9 kWh-per-consumer-per-degree coefficient documented here will not decrease on its own — urbanisation, rising incomes, and a warming climate ensure it moves only in one direction. The question is whether the planning system runs ahead of the demand curve or continues to discover constraints at 2 AM in June when Lahore hits 47°C and the grid is drawing 4,100 MW at midnight with no room to flex.
Section 7

Methodology and data quality notes

Primary data sources

  • ISMO (CPPA-G) monthly operational reports: "Marginal Price/Cost vs Demand" files, covering Feb 2024 – Apr 2026 (26 months). Contains hourly LESCO demand, KE CPPA demand, system marginal cost, and generation mix.
  • NEPRA Fuel Cost Adjustment decisions: July 2024 FCA for KE (KE own generation 1,076 GWh/month). KE Multi-Year Tariff determination FY2023-24 (17,768 GWh total KE dispatch: 48% CPPA, 42% own gen, 9.9% private).
  • Climatological monthly mean temperatures: Pakistan Met Dept long-run averages for Lahore and Karachi (stable proxies; actual 2024 monthly means within 1–2°C of normals).

Known data quality issues

  • Jun-24 / Jul-24 identical: ISMO source files show identical data for June and July 2024 across LESCO and KE demand sheets — a copy-paste anomaly in the upstream file. Jul-24 is excluded from analysis; Jun-24 is used as the single summer 2024 peak representation.
  • May–Sep 2025 missing: ISMO public database does not contain these five months. This gap omits the June 2025 Lahore extreme heat event (46°C on June 9 — hottest in 18 years). Analysis is limited to 2024 summer data as the peak heat proxy.
  • KE own generation estimated: KE own-gen figures (1,447 MW Jun-24) are derived from NEPRA FCA data, not metered directly. Seasonal profile estimated from the known annual average (851 MW from NEPRA MYT) and the July 2024 peak (1,447 MW). Marked [Est.] throughout.
  • Consumer counts: LESCO 5.9M total (Grokipedia, Oct 2025 AMI procurement document) × 88% = 5.2M domestic. KE 3.7M total (Wikipedia, NEPRA MYT) × 87% = 3.2M domestic. Exact category breakdown unavailable without full NEPRA SoI 2024 consumer census.

Regression methodology

OLS regression of monthly average system demand (MW) against monthly climatological mean temperature (°C) for nine 2024 months (Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov). Conversion to per-consumer: slope (MW/°C) × 1,000 kW/MW × 720 hrs/month ÷ domestic consumer count. Per-day equivalent: ÷ 30 days. Jul-24 excluded (duplicate). Energy delta at system generation level; actual consumer meter readings are ~15–16% lower after T&D losses (LESCO 15.92% T&D loss rate per NEPRA PER FY2024-25; KE 14.73%).
