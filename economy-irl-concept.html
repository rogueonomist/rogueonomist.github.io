The Concept
Economics as a decoding tool, not a subject

This should not feel like an economics programme made for children. It should feel like a youth show in which economics becomes the tool used to decode real life — pocket money, gaming, careers, AI, influencers, inequality, inflation and Pakistan's future.

The strongest format is a father-daughter investigation and challenge show. The daughter represents the intelligent, sceptical viewer who refuses to accept complicated or evasive answers. The father brings the economics, evidence and wider context — but she determines whether the explanation actually makes sense.

That relationship is the show's greatest asset. Younger audiences particularly value relatable people, authentic relationships and genuine disagreement over manufactured aspiration. The programme should feel conversational, visual and participatory rather than like a television lecture.

The central tension: Two people who love each other, think differently, and refuse to pretend the other is automatically right. The daughter sometimes proves the economist wrong. That is not a bug — that is the show.

A recurring Jargon Alarm goes off whenever terms like "fiscal deficit," "monetary transmission" or "capital formation" are used without plain explanation. The economist then has 20 seconds to explain using an example from her life. A running score tracks this across all six episodes — by the finale, she has a number.

Crucially: she sometimes proves him wrong, changes his mind, or makes a better decision than him in the Choice Lab. That outcome should be allowed to stand, uncorrected and unqualified.

The Editorial Promise
"You will not leave every episode knowing the correct answer. You will leave knowing how to think about the question."

The show never begins with "Today we are going to learn about..." It begins with a provocation — "This does not make sense," "I think adults have got this wrong," "Can I actually become rich doing this?" That framing respects young people as thinkers rather than recipients of information.

Why this dynamic works

A 13-year-old in Karachi sees someone like them — curious, impatient with jargon, unimpressed by credentials — actually winning arguments against a qualified economist on television. That is both entertaining and quietly transformative.

Episode Structure
Six chapters. 24–26 minutes. Every episode.

Each episode follows a recognisable structure signposted clearly on screen. Viewers always know where they are in the investigation. The structure can be made familiar enough to become a ritual — and flexible enough to accommodate very different topics.

01 60–90 sec
Cold Open
"Would You Do It?"
Begin with a surprising decision, not a definition. Both hosts commit to an answer before the investigation begins — before either knows the economics. This immediately divides the audience into two camps and creates genuine stakes. No textbook framing. No context-setting. A provocation first.

"Would you rather receive PKR 10,000 today or PKR 1,000 every month for a year?"

"Would you pay PKR 500,000 for a university degree if AI could do the same job?"

"Should the government just print money to pay for hospitals?"

Device — Commitment before knowledge. Stakes before explanation.
02 4–5 min
Field Investigation
The Real-Life Test
Leave the studio. Test the episode question in a setting young people recognise. Instead of telling viewers that prices have risen, give the daughter the same money and ask her to recreate a basket of goods from three years ago. The gap is visible, physical and impossible to dismiss.

Locations: school canteen · supermarket · gaming café · university campus · shopping mall · public transport stop · small online business · street market · sports ground

Device — Experience before explanation. Show the problem before naming it.
03 3–4 min
The Explanation
"Dad, Prove It"
The economist now explains what is actually happening, using exactly one strong visual device. Never more than one major chart or idea on screen at a time. The daughter can trigger the Jargon Alarm at any point. If she does, the 20-second clock appears and the explanation restarts from her life.

Devices: physical coins or blocks · animated map · price timeline · game board · scale showing winners and losers · hand-drawn diagram · receipt wall · simple "money moving" sketch

Device — One idea. One visual. Fully explained before the next one begins.
04 5–6 min
Youth Decision
The Choice Lab
Four recurring youth panellists — approximately 13, 15, 17 and 20 years old — face an actual economic decision under time pressure. They are given a problem to solve, not a microphone for opinions. Every choice has a visible consequence revealed on screen. The age range means different maturity levels appear naturally without anyone being condescended to.

"You have PKR 100 billion. Allocate it between schools, hospitals, roads, climate resilience and lower taxes — and defend every allocation."

Device — Stakes, consequences, visible trade-offs. Decision first, analysis after.
05 3 min
Depth Layer
Level Up
The story that began with pocket money now escalates to a larger economic framework. The "Level Up" graphic signals that younger viewers can stay with the example while older viewers get the underlying theory. A story about saving pocket money becomes a discussion of compound growth, opportunity cost and risk — without losing either audience.

Concepts: opportunity cost · compound growth · incentives · inequality · public goods · risk · taxation · inflation · human capital · productivity · market failure

Device — Layered depth. Single escalation. No viewer left behind.
06 2 min
The Verdict
"What Changed My Mind?"
Return to the cold-open question. Each host says what they thought at the beginning and what they think now. It is perfectly acceptable for neither to have changed their minds — the segment demonstrates thoughtful disagreement rather than forcing a tidy resolution. The episode ends with a viewer poll question for the following week. The Before/After poll shift, tracked via WhatsApp and social channels, becomes the show's evidence of its own learning impact.
Device — Honest revision. No forced consensus. Disagreement treated as a valid outcome.
Six-Episode Arc
From the individual to the country

The series moves deliberately from personal economics (how money affects my life today) to future economics (what the economy will look like in 2035) to national economics (how Pakistan works and who gets to shape it). Expand any episode card for the full brief.

Episode 01  ·  Individual
Why Does Everything Cost More?
"Why does the same money buy less than it did three years ago?"
Inflation Purchasing Power Exchange Rates Expectations
Cold Open
"Would you rather have PKR 10,000 today — or PKR 10,000 that bought what it bought in 2022?"
Real-Life Test
The daughter takes a 2022 school canteen receipt and tries to recreate the identical basket at today's prices. The difference is chalked live on the Pakistan Price Index wall — a studio fixture that grows across all six episodes.
Choice Lab
Youth panel picks one policy: control the exchange rate, subsidise food prices, or raise civil servant salaries. Each choice triggers a visible consequence chain. No correct answer exists.
Level Up
Why central banks exist. How price expectations can cause inflation before a single rupee is printed. What Pakistan's inflation history reveals about institutional trust.
Villain of the Episode
Hoarding. How it works, who benefits and why price controls usually make it worse.
+ Full brief
Episode 02  ·  Individual
Spend It or Save It?
"Is enjoying your money today actually irresponsible?"
Opportunity Cost Compound Growth Debt Risk
Cold Open
"You have PKR 10,000. Spend it on an experience, invest it, save it, donate it, or start something. Which one is the adult move?"
Real-Life Test
Three 16-year-olds each received PKR 5,000 six months ago. One spent it, one saved it in a bank, one traded it into something. We find out what actually happened — numbers only, no judgment.
Level Up
Compound interest as the most underrated idea in personal finance. Why Pakistan's inflation rate changes the calculus. The psychology of delayed gratification — and when refusing it is actually the rational choice.
Sana Thread (Ep 2)
Sana decides to put her PKR 5,000 into a small side hustle rather than save it. We record the decision. The consequence will appear in episode 4.
+ Full brief
Episode 03  ·  Future
What Will You Be Worth in 2035?
"Which skills will still matter when AI becomes better at most things?"
AI & Automation Human Capital Wages Education
Cold Open
"Would you pay PKR 500,000 for a university degree if an AI assistant could do the same job for free?"
Real-Life Test
Four tasks — writing, coding, financial analysis, pastoral counselling. The daughter attempts each. Then an AI tool attempts each. The youth panel decides which result they would actually pay for, and why.
Level Up
Why technology has historically created more jobs than it destroys — and why Pakistan's structural situation may produce a different outcome. What "complementary skills" means in practice versus on paper.
Villain of the Episode
Credential inflation: the economic mechanism by which more degrees produce lower graduate wages.
+ Full brief
Episode 04  ·  Future
Can the Internet Make You Rich?
"Are creators, gamers and online sellers running real businesses — or expensive hobbies?"
Platform Economics Revenue vs Profit Entrepreneurship Algorithms
Cold Open
"A creator has 200,000 followers and posts every day. Are they building a business — or an expensive hobby?"
Real-Life Test
Help an actual young creator or online seller price their first digital product. Walk through revenue, platform fees, taxes and what the profit actually looks like after six months. Sana's side hustle from episode 2 reappears here with real numbers.
Level Up
Platform dependency risk. How algorithms create winners and quietly disappear them. The difference between income and wealth — and why the creator economy often produces the former without the latter.
Your Rupee, Your Call
A real online seller with PKR 50,000 to deploy: the youth panel decides between marketing, product development and operations. The outcome is followed up in episode 5.
+ Full brief
Episode 05  ·  National
Is the Economy Fair?
"Why do equally talented people end up in such different places?"
Inequality Social Mobility Taxation Privilege
Cold Open
"Two people have identical talent and identical work ethic. One was born in Clifton. One was born in Lyari. At 30, who earns more — and by how much? Is that difference economics, or injustice, or both?"
Real-Life Test: The Inequality Game
Youth panel members start with different tokens: income, education access, connections, health. They make identical decisions. The diverging outcomes are made visible. No one is blamed. The mechanism is shown.
Level Up
Why inequality slows economic growth — not just hurts individuals. Gini coefficients, intergenerational mobility and the specific things social protection can and cannot fix.
Your Rupee, Your Call (follow-up)
Results from the episode 4 seller decision revealed. Did the youth panel's allocation work? Real data, not simulation.

Episode 06  ·  Finale
You Run Pakistan
"What would young people do differently if they controlled the national budget?"
Public Spending Debt Climate Trade-offs
Format
The youth panel receives a simulated national budget. Every decision updates a live national dashboard on screen: more subsidies reduce education funding; lower taxes reduce revenue; climate investment reduces future disaster costs but raises current borrowing; skills investment raises future productivity but shows no immediate impact on the dashboard.
The Sana Scorecard
Sana's six decisions across the series are shown together for the first time. Her actual trajectory is compared to the two most common alternative paths. The compounding effect is visible. No moral is stated — the graphic speaks.
Participants
All recurring panellists return. Guests from earlier episodes observe and react. Every cold-open question from episodes 1–5 reappears attached to a real budget line number.
The Ending
The series ends on agency rather than pessimism. The economy is not something that merely happens to young people. It is a system they will inherit — and can improve. The final question goes to the audience, not to the hosts.

Recurring Segments
Identifiable devices that give the show character

New additions to the original format

Six new recurring devices have been developed beyond the original brief to add continuity, measurability and real-world stakes.

Pakistan Price Index Villain of the Episode The Sana Thread WhatsApp Before / After Your Rupee, Your Call Age Breakdown Cards Economy IRL Classroom
🔔 The Jargon Alarm
The daughter stops any explanation that sounds like a textbook. A 20-second timer appears. The economist must re-explain using an example from her life. Failures are scored across all six episodes. By the finale she holds a running total — and it appears on screen.
🧾 Receipts, Please
One of you makes a factual claim. The other demands evidence. The number appears on screen with a visible source citation in the corner — Dawn-style credibility applied to every factual statement. No claim floats without a reference. This also models good epistemic practice for young viewers.
💸 Rich, Broke or Just Bad Economics?
Examine a viral claim, business idea or money trend and classify it as a genuinely good idea, bad luck, or structurally wrong. The show's connection to the internet's live conversation. Keeps the content evergreen for digital clips and gives the format a reason to engage with current social media discourse without chasing it.
📊 The Tier List
Rank careers, investments, government policies or spending choices from sensible to disastrous. A directly familiar online format — YouTube reports over a billion views of tier-list content — applied to economics decisions. Inherently social and clippable. Each episode produces at least one tier-list clip built for sharing.
⏱️ One Minute, No Big Words
An expert — entrepreneur, policymaker, creator or banker — has exactly 60 seconds to answer a youth panel question without using professional jargon. If they use a jargon term, the penalty clock continues. Clean, punchy and highly clippable. Senior officials are welcome but must answer the same way as everyone else.
🛒 Pakistan Price Index New
A physical wall in the studio tracks a basket of 10 everyday goods — a school meal, a haircut, a 2GB data plan, a litre of petrol, a textbook, a bus ticket, a kilo of atta. Updated at the top of every episode. By the finale it is a physical record of what six episodes of Pakistan's economy looked like in real time. It anchors every inflation discussion to lived experience.
⚖️ Villain of the Episode New
One bad economic idea goes on trial each week: hoarding, pyramid schemes, circular debt, price-fixing, informal sector evasion, energy theft. The youth panel acts as jury. Arguments for and against are heard. A verdict is delivered. The economic mechanism of the harm is then explained without lecturing. Combines genuine drama with economic education.
📱 WhatsApp Before / After New
One week before the episode, the cold-open question goes out on Dawn's WhatsApp channel and social platforms. Results are revealed at the cold open. After the episode, the same question is sent again. The shift in the audience's answer is displayed — that shift is the measurable learning the show produced. Creates a pre-broadcast audience relationship and gives advertisers a learning metric.
💰 Your Rupee, Your Call New
Each episode, one viewer with a real small decision and real stakes receives advice from the youth panel. A street vendor with PKR 30,000 deciding between a new cart and digital payments. A freelancer choosing between two client offers. The outcome is followed up in the next episode. This is not a prize — it is a real consequence, and the follow-up makes that clear.
🎯 Age Breakdown Cards New
At the end of every episode, three distinct "what can you do with this now" cards appear — one for ages 12–15, one for 16–18, one for 19–25. No one is talked down to. Each card is at the right level for where that viewer actually is in life. A 13-year-old and a 22-year-old watching the same episode each leave with a different and appropriate action.
👤 The Sana Thread New
A fictional 17-year-old named Sana makes one economic decision per episode. A simple graphic tracks her trajectory. By the finale, her six decisions have compounded into a visible outcome, compared to two alternative paths. Creates the narrative continuity a topic-by-topic format otherwise lacks. Keeps recurring viewers invested across all six episodes.
🎓 Economy IRL Classroom New
Schools register to watch episodes as a class and submit one collective question per episode. One classroom question is selected, addressed on screen and the school is named. Gives teachers a ready-made economics tool. Gives the show a direct link to Pakistan's 53 million school-age population. Creates earned media and institutional partnerships at zero cost.
Production Strategy
Television first. Digital by design.

The programme is produced as television but structured so that every episode simultaneously becomes a content library. The clips do not merely promote the full episode — each one must contain a complete question, reveal or useful idea on its own terms.

From every episode

  • Broadcast One 24–26-minute episode
  • YouTube One 6–8-minute central investigation cut
  • Vertical Four or five 30–90-second clips
  • Social One WhatsApp / social poll (pre- and post-air)
  • Clip One Jargon Alarm moment
  • Clip One Villain of the Episode verdict
  • Clip One host disagreement (unresolved)
  • Edu One classroom challenge card
  • Edu Three Age Breakdown Cards (12–15, 16–18, 19–25)

Guest philosophy

Avoid conventional television panels. A 13-year-old will lose interest in three adults behind a desk within 90 seconds. Every guest must be brought in because they can show, build, test or decide something — not because they hold a title.

  • Good A teenage entrepreneur explaining their first loss
  • Good A creator showing views-to-income breakdown live
  • Good A climate scientist allocating a city budget
  • Good A shopkeeper explaining pricing decisions in real time
  • Good A university student with PKR 3,000 left this month
  • Avoid Officials delivering pre-prepared economic philosophy
  • Avoid Panels with all guests in the same age bracket

Audience by segment

  • Ages 12–15 Cold open and Choice Lab most engaging
  • Ages 16–18 Level Up and Sana Thread; planning horizon matches
  • Ages 19–25 Villain of the Episode and national policy debate
  • Parents Joint-viewing hook: they are watching themselves
  • Teachers Economy IRL Classroom programme; 53M school-age Pakistanis

The WhatsApp Before/After poll creates a measurable before-and-after for every episode — the shift in audience answers is the show's evidence of its own impact.

Visual and editorial language

Use
  • Bold, readable typography
  • Mobile-style choice cards
  • Countdown clocks
  • Animated receipts
  • Game-board consequences
  • Hand-drawn annotations
Avoid
  • Cartoon coins and excessive emojis
  • Adults imitating TikTok personalities
  • Fake youth slang
  • Multiple charts per segment
  • Panel shows with no physical activity
  • Manufactured positivity or forced resolution
The editorial rule that overrides everything else
Never begin with "Today we are going to learn about..." Begin with a problem, a contradiction, a bad decision someone already made, or a question the audience did not know they had. Respect produces engagement. Condescension produces a channel change.
